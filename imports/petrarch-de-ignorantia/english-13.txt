So the secrets of nature, and, higher than these, the mysteries of God, which we receive in humble faith, these men strive to snatch at with a proud swagger; and they do not reach them, do not even come near them, but in their madness reckon that they have reached them and are gripping heaven in their fist;
and it comes to the same thing for them as if they were, since they are satisfied with their own opinion and delighted by their mistake.
And nothing draws them back from this madness: not, I say, the impossibility of the thing itself, put in the apostle's words to the Romans, "For who has known the mind of the Lord, or who has been his counsellor?", nor that heavenly advice in Ecclesiasticus:
"Do not seek what is too high for you, and do not search out what is too strong for you; but think always on what God has commanded you, and do not be inquisitive about the greater part of his works, for you have no need to see the things that are hidden."
I do not press these texts; they despise equally whatever they know to have been said from heaven, or rather, to say what is the case, whatever they know to have been said as a Catholic.
But there is at least Democritus' not unclever joke: "nobody looks at what is in front of his feet; they scrutinise the regions of the sky";
and that most amusing mockery by Cicero of men who argue rashly and doubt about nothing, "as though they had just come down from the council of the gods" and had seen with their own eyes and heard with their own ears what goes on there;
or that older and sharper example: in Homer, Jupiter with a heavy threat warns off, not a mortal man, and not some god from the common herd, but Juno herself, his wife and sister and queen of the gods,
from daring to ask after his innermost secret or presuming that it could be known.
But let us come back to Aristotle, by whose brilliance bleary and weak eyes are dazzled, so that many have already slipped into the pits of error.
I know that he laid down the unity of rule, which Homer had laid down before him. For Homer says, in the prose Latin in which it has come to us: "Rule by many is not good: let there be one lord, one commander."
And Aristotle: "A plurality of rules is not good, therefore one ruler." But Homer meant human rule, Aristotle divine; Homer the rule of the Greeks, Aristotle of all things; Homer set up the son of Atreus as ruler, Aristotle God: so far did the flash of the truth light up his mind.
But who this ruler is, and of what kind, and how great, I believe he did not know; and I believe that a man who had argued so very inquisitively about a mass of trifles failed to see this one greatest thing, which many who know no letters have seen and do see, by no other light,
though it shines on them differently.
And if those friends of mine do not see that this is so, I see that they are blind and have plainly had their eyes put out; and I should no more hesitate about its seeming so to everyone whose eyes are sound than about the emerald being green, snow white, the raven black.
And so that our Aristotelians may bear my boldness more calmly: I do not think this of one man only, though I name only one. I read, ignorant as I am, and before these men detected my ignorance I used to seem to understand something.
I read, I say, though in greener years I read more attentively. Even now I read the books of the poets and philosophers, and Cicero's before all others, in whose mind and style I have taken particular delight ever since I was a boy.
I find in him a great deal of eloquence and the greatest force of elegant language.
But as regards the gods themselves, on whose nature he published books under that title, and as regards everything to do with religion, the more eloquently it is said, the emptier the tale is to me;
and I silently give thanks to God, who gave me this mind, sluggish or modest as it may be, and a spirit that does not wander, does not seek things above itself, and is not inquisitive about searching into matters that are hard to look for and deadly to find,
but such that the more I hear said against the faith of Christ, the more I love Christ and the firmer I stand in his faith.
It happens with me as it does with a man whose love for his father has cooled, who then hears people speaking against him: the love that seemed asleep at once catches fire. So it must turn out, if he is truly a son.
Often, and I call Christ himself to witness, the blasphemies of heretics have made me from a Christian the most Christian of men. Those old pagans, though they tell many stories about the gods, do not blaspheme, because they have no knowledge of the true God;
they had not heard the name of Christ, and faith comes from hearing; and although "their sound has gone out into all the earth, and their words to the ends of the world",
yet by the time the words and teachings of the apostles were sounding through the whole world they were already dead and buried: to be pitied rather than blamed, since the grudging earth had already stopped up the ears with which they might have drunk in the faith that saves.
