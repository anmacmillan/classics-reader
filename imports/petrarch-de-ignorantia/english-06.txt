First they said that public repute stood on my side; but they answered that it deserved little credit, and there they did not lie, since the crowd very rarely sees the truth.
Next they said that the friendships of the greatest and most learned men, adorned by which, and here I glory in the Lord, I have lived my life, told against their verdict.
And so too the intimacy of many kings, and in particular of Robert, king of Sicily, who honoured me even as a young man with repeated and public testimony to my learning and my ability.
They answered — and here they plainly lied to themselves, out of vanity, I will not say out of injustice — that the king had a great reputation for letters but no real knowledge of them;
and that the rest, learned though they were, had judged me without sufficient penetration, whether from affection or from carelessness.
They further put it to themselves that the three most recent Roman pontiffs had each in turn competed to call me, though in vain, to a distinguished place in their household, and that the present incumbent, Urban,
has been in the habit of speaking well of me and has already written to me in the kindest terms; and further, that the Roman emperor, for there has been no other lawful emperor in this age, counts me among his dear intimates,
and is accustomed to summon me to him daily with much pressing of entreaties, repeated messengers and letters. This is widely known and doubted by no one. From it, they realise, some argument for my being worth something might be drawn.
But they disposed of this objection too, maintaining that the popes had either followed report and gone astray with everyone else, or had been moved to act by my character and not by my learning, and that the emperor had been moved by his interest in past events and in history,
of which they do not deny me some knowledge.
Besides this, they said that eloquence told against them, which, so help me, I do not recognise in myself, though they report me an effective enough persuader; for although the business of the rhetorician or orator is to speak fitly in order to persuade,
and his end is to persuade by speaking, they say this has fallen to many unlearned men as well. What belongs to art they assign to luck, and they bring out the common saying, "much eloquence, little wisdom";
and they do not notice that Cato's famous definition of the orator stands against this slander.
Lastly, they said my written style told against them; and being afraid not merely to criticise it but even to praise it sparingly, they admitted it was thoroughly elegant and uncommon, but without any learning behind it.
How that can be I do not understand, nor do I think they understand it; and I suppose that if they come to themselves and think over what they said, they will be ashamed of so pointless an absurdity.
For if the first were true, which again I neither admit nor believe, I should not doubt that the second was false. How should a man ignorant of everything have an excellent style, when they, who are ignorant of nothing, have none at all?
Are we then, suspecting that everything happens by chance, to leave no room for reason?
