As for me, my friend, so that you may know everything, and may know from where and in what spirit I write this to you: I am sitting in a small boat among the eddies of the Po.
Do not be surprised if either the hand that writes or the speech itself wavers. I am sailing up this huge river against the current, with the whole of my ignorance aboard: a river on whose banks I once wrote much as a young man and thought out much that won the approval of the old men of those days,
before these young men detected an old man's ignorance. How unsteady is the lot of men.
The Po itself seemed in a way to pity me, as though it remembered my studies and knew my old concerns, because the man it saw as a young man in glory — if that can be said without pride — it now sees as an old man without glory, stripped of the shining garments of fame;
and it keeps driving me back with a great rush and its whole flood, to reclaim my rights from unjust judges.
But I, who loathe this burden of fame, laborious to me and envied by the very men I least suspected, avoiding lawsuits and despising contempt, leave my spoils to my dear plunderers.
Let them keep it, since I give way, if fame, like money, passes to the robber when it is snatched from its owner. Let them keep either knowledge, or what among fools counts as equal to it, the reputation for knowledge.
I go stripped of both, or certainly of the second, that is of the reputation for knowledge; happier perhaps and richer in my humble nakedness than they are in their proud spoils, which, as I judge, are not theirs.
So I go gladly, having laid down a bright and heavy bundle, and I overcome the resisting Po with oars, sails and tow-ropes, making again for Pavia, that old and studious city, where I shall certainly not find the old garment of fame that was lost among the sailors,
if that were my concern; but I shall not be able to do without it, however much I wish to. For my part I shall always strive and pray to be called unlettered, so long as I am a good man or not a bad one, so that at least in that way I may have rest: to a tired man nothing is sweeter than rest.
A lying reputation for letters, as I now hear, has snatched that rest from me until this day; and a reputation for ignorance, whether false or true, will give it back. And so, late as it is, at last all will be well.
Yet I am afraid I shall strive and pray for it in vain.
So many think against my judges that wherever they publish this verdict of theirs, not only where I am now travelling but anywhere in this world of ours, although with me it has already passed into a settled matter, as you have heard,
with others it will fall back on their own heads, by the judgment of more and greater men; except perhaps in that one city, most noble and best, where they dare to say such things,
where, because of the size of the population and its manifold variety, there are many who philosophise and pass judgment without letters.
For there is great liberty in everything, and, what I should call the one great evil there, far too much liberty of words; relying on which the silliest of men often insult distinguished names, to the indignation of good men,
who are also so numerous there that I doubt whether any city has so many good and modest men; but everywhere the line of fools is so much larger that the indignation of the wise counts for nothing.
The name of liberty is so sweet to everyone that rashness and effrontery please the crowd, because they look like it.
Hence owls provoke the eagle with impunity, crows the swan, monkeys the lion; hence the foul tear at the honourable, the ignorant at the learned, cowards at the brave, the bad at the good;
and good men do not stand in the way of the licence of the bad, because the bad outnumber them and have public favour on their side, since people believe it is expedient that whatever one likes to say should also be allowed.
So thoroughly has that saying of Tiberius Caesar taken root, that in a free city the tongue and the mind ought to be free. Free they certainly ought to be, but on terms that the freedom is free of injury.
