Indeed, in this great poverty of knowledge, where human pride spreads unfledged wings to the wind, how frequent and how hard the reefs are. How many and how ridiculous the vanities of those who philosophise. What contradiction of opinions, what obstinacy,
what impudence. What a number of sects, what disagreements, what wars, what ambiguity in the matter, what tangle in the words. How deep and how unapproachable the hiding places of the truth,
how many the ambushes of sophists, who with every effort block the road to truth with brambles as it were, so that one cannot make out which path runs straighter to it. For which reason Cato the elder, as we know, moved that Carneades be driven out of the city.
And last of all, what rashness on the one hand, and on the other what mistrust in the greatest men, and a kind of despair of ever grasping the truth.
Pythagoras says that on every subject one may argue both ways with equal arguments, and on this very point too, whether every subject is equally arguable.
There are those who say that the truth is deeply buried and sunk as in a deep well: as though truth had to be fetched out of the lowest hiding places of the earth rather than from the highest summit of heaven, and dragged up with hooks and ropes,
and not approached by the ladders of grace and the steps of the mind. Socrates says: "This one thing I know, that I know nothing." And Arcesilaus criticised this most humble profession of ignorance as too bold, maintaining that not even this one thing is known, that nothing is known.
There is your glorious philosophy, which either professes ignorance or at least forbids the knowledge of ignorance. What a two-headed piece of quackery. What an inextricable game.
On the other side Gorgias of Leontini, that most ancient rhetorician, believes that not merely something but everything can be known, and not only by the philosopher but by the orator; for, as Cicero says, he thought the orator could speak excellently on every subject,
which he certainly could not do himself; and he could not speak excellently on every subject unless he knew every subject excellently. Hermagoras thought the same, who assigned to the orator not only rhetoric but the whole of philosophy and the knowledge of everything.
A great confidence in a middling mind. But far bolder than all of them was Hippias, who dared to profess that he knew everything, so as to claim full glory for himself not only in the liberal studies and in the whole of philosophy but in the mechanical arts as well.
I should call him a divine man, if I did not believe him mad.
But since it is now certain that not everything, indeed not even much, can be known by a man, and since the Academy has long ago been refuted and hissed off, and it is established that with God's revealing something can be known, let it be enough to know as much as suffices for salvation.
Many have perished from being wiser than they should be, and, saying they were wise, as the Apostle says, "they became fools, and their senseless heart was darkened."
If it falls to me to be wise unto sobriety, which the unlettered company of saints of both sexes shows can happen without much learning, indeed without any, that will be enough, and I shall reckon that things go happily with me,
and I shall never regret my studies; and these chattering know-nothings, swollen with a false name for letters, who love to be called what they are not, I shall either pity or hate or laugh at as they wrangle about empty and unknown matters,
and I shall not envy them their boasting, nor their deadly swelling, nor anything at all, and certainly not their riches: men who never come back to themselves, who are always poured out abroad and looking for themselves outside themselves.
In the end I gladly lay down the name of a lettered man, and have laid it down already: if it was undeserved, to satisfy truth and conscience; and if not, to satisfy envy. As for the fact of the matter, let posterity see to it, if I get that far on the feet of fame; and if not, oblivion will.
Let an uncorrupted posterity see to it, I say, which no disturbance of feeling, no hatred, no anger, no love, and no spite, those enemies of truth, will keep from judging fairly.
Let posterity see to it, if it knows me; my judges it will certainly not recognise, since not even this age knows them, scarcely known as they are to their own neighbourhood.
Let it see and judge; and if it approves their verdict, I accept it. If it quashes the verdict, I am not therefore angry with these men, knowing how great a power the passions have in the minds of men.
These, then, are the men who dictated this sentence against me. I am wrong: there was one, whom I have named often today, spite. Spite wrote it with its own fingers, and neither love nor reason could change it.
Why then should I be angry with my friends for something committed by their enemy?
If a father does not bear the guilt of his son, nor a son of his father, how much less should an enemy's wrongdoing harm a friend, especially a friend bound in that enemy's prison and chains, who, if he is ever given back to himself, will avenge both his own injuries and his friend's.
