So it has turned out with me as with Laberius.
After my sixtieth year I have fallen from my station: not, like him, into being a mime — a trade which, low as it is, still calls for a clever practitioner and holds its own place among the mechanical arts — but into what is lowest of all, into being ignorant.
So things go: my studies and my labours and my sleepless nights have come to this, that I, who as a young man used to be called learned by some, am found by a more searching judgment to be, as an old man, a know-nothing.
Something to grieve over, perhaps, but to be borne; perhaps not even to be grieved over, but certainly to be borne, like everything else that befalls men: loss, poverty, toil, pain, weariness, death, exile, disgrace.
If the disgrace is false it should be despised, for it will meet with contradiction and will wear out as it travels; if true, it should not be refused, any more than the other punishments devised for men's faults. For my part, if a true distinction in learning is taken from me by words, I shall laugh.
But if it was a false distinction, I shall not merely bear the loss but be glad of it, shaken free of baggage not my own and released from the laborious guarding of an undeserved reputation. A robber is better treated when he is stripped of unjust plunder than when he enjoys his theft unpunished.
The man who evicts an unjust owner may himself be unjust, but the eviction is just all the same. As far as I am concerned, as I said, I accept not only a just sentence but an unjust one, and I refuse neither any judge nor any plunderer.
Reputation is a laborious and difficult thing, and reputation in letters above all.
Everyone is awake and under arms against it; even those who cannot hope for it themselves try to snatch it from those who have it. The pen must always be in your hand; you must stand always in the line of battle with your mind alert and your ears pricked.
Whoever frees me from these cares and this bundle, with whatever intention, has my gratitude as my deliverer; and whether the name of a lettered man is false or true, it is certainly laborious and anxious, and, greedy as I am for rest and leisure, I lay it down gladly,
remembering that saying of Seneca: this praise costs a great outlay of time and great annoyance to other people's ears, "what a lettered man!" Let us be content with the more rustic title, "what a good man!"
I stand by your advice, best of teachers of morals. I am content with the more rustic title, as you call it — better and holier, as I judge, and for that reason nobler as well — especially since it is the one my judges leave me.
Yet I am afraid, as I was saying, that this very title may be false.
Still I strive to make it true, and I shall not give this up or grow tired of it until my last breath and my final gasp; and if, as you said elsewhere, willing it is what is needed for me to be good, then whether the willing completes the work or only begins it, it will be well,
and wanting to become good is itself a part of goodness: for that part at least I hope the title is true.
