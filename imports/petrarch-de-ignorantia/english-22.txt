In this whole class of question — to come back at last, late as it is, to where I left off, for I have been carried along by a chain of connected matters — Aristotle is the one most to be avoided, not because he has more errors, but because he has more authority and more followers.
Perhaps they will admit, compelled either by the truth or by shame, that Aristotle did not see divine things well enough, nor eternal ones, since these lie beyond unaided intelligence; but they will insist that there was nothing among human and passing things that he did not see through.
So we come back to what Macrobius says, arguing against this very philosopher, whether in jest or in earnest: "It seems to me that so great a man could not have been ignorant of anything."
To me the exact opposite seems true; nor would I grant that any man ever had knowledge of all things by human study. For this I am torn to pieces; and although the root of the envy is something else, this is the reason put forward: that I do not adore Aristotle.
But I have another to adore, who promises me not empty and frivolous guesses about deceiving things, useless and resting on no foundation, but the knowledge of himself;
and if he grants that, then as for all the other things he founded, adding them will seem superfluous, grasping them easy, and inquiring into them ridiculous.
Him then I have to hope in; him I have to adore; and I wish my judges worshipped him devoutly too. If they do, they know that the philosophers have told many lies, I mean those who are called philosophers, for true philosophers are in the habit of speaking nothing but the truth.
Yet neither Aristotle is of that number, certainly, nor Plato, whom our own philosophers have said came nearer to the truth than any of that ancient line of philosophers.
But these men, as I said, are so captivated by love of the name alone that they count it a sacrilege to say anything on any subject differently from him.
This is their chief proof of my ignorance: that I said something or other about virtue differently and not sufficiently in Aristotle's manner. There is a crime that deserves crucifixion.
It is quite possible that I said not merely something different but something contrary, and did not therefore say it badly, being bound to swear by no master's words, as Horace says of himself.
It is also possible that I said the same thing, though in different words, and only seemed to say something else to men who judge everything without understanding everything.
For a great many ignorant people cling to words as a shipwrecked man to a plank, and do not think a thing can be well said in more than one way: such is their poverty either of understanding or of the speech by which conceptions are expressed.
For my part I admit that I do not much delight in that man's style, in the form we have it, although I learned, on the testimony of the Greeks and the authority of Cicero, that in his own language he was sweet and copious and ornate,
and I learned that before I was condemned by a verdict of ignorance.
But through the crudeness or the grudging of translators he has reached us harsh and rough, so that he can neither fully please the ear nor stick in the memory;
and so it happens that sometimes to express Aristotle's meaning not in his words but in one's own is both more agreeable to the listener and readier for the speaker.
