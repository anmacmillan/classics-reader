Among them all, however, those three books of Cicero entitled On the Nature of the Gods, which I mentioned above, stir me most powerfully and often.
There that great mind, dealing with the gods, often mocks and looks down on the gods themselves, not openly, perhaps for fear of punishment, which even the apostles feared before the coming of the Holy Spirit,
but with those most telling jokes he has in abundance, which make it clear to the discerning what he thought about the very subject he had taken up; so that, often pitying his lot as I read,
I sigh silently to myself in grief that the man did not know the true God. He had died a few years before the birth of Christ, and death had closed those eyes for which, so close at hand, the end of a wandering night and of darkness, and the beginning of truth,
and the dawn of the true light and the sun of righteousness were at hand.
Yet this same Cicero, in the countless books he wrote, though carried along by the torrent of common errors he often names gods, still often mocks them, as I said; and from his youth onwards, when writing the books On Invention,
he had said that those who apply themselves to philosophy do not think there are gods. To know God and not gods is indeed the true and highest philosophy: that is, provided piety and faithful worship are added to the knowledge.
The same man, when older, in these very books which he writes about gods and not about God, gathers himself and lifts himself on such wings of intellect that at times you would think it was not a pagan philosopher speaking but an apostle;
as in this passage in the first book, against Velleius, the defender of the Epicurean view: "You were finding fault," he says, "with those who, from magnificent and splendid works, when they had seen the world itself and its parts, the sky, the lands, the seas,
and their glories, the sun and the moon and the stars, and when they had come to know the ripenings of the seasons and their changes and turns, suspected that there was some excellent and surpassing nature which had made these things, and moved them,
and ruled and steered them." And in the second book: "What can be so obvious and so clear, once we have looked at the sky and contemplated the heavenly bodies, as that there is some divine power of surpassing mind by which these things are governed?"
And in the same book: "Chrysippus," he says, "though he has the keenest of minds, says things which he seems to have learned from nature herself rather than to have discovered for himself. 'For if,' he says, 'there is anything in the nature of things which the mind and reason of man,
which the strength and capacity of man, cannot bring about, then certainly what brought it about is better than man.
And heavenly things, and all those whose order is everlasting, cannot be made by man; therefore that by which they are made is better than man. And what would you rather call it than God?'" Then, after a few lines,
"But if all the parts of the world," he says, "are so arranged that they could not have been made better for use or more beautiful in appearance, let us see whether this is by chance, or whether they could in no way have held together in the condition they are in
without a directing intelligence and divine providence.
If, then, the things completed by nature are better than those completed by art, and art produces nothing without reason, then nature too must not be held to be without reason.
So it will not do, when you have looked at a statue or a painted panel, to know that art was applied to it, and when you see a ship's course far off, not to doubt that it is moved by reason and art,
or when you study a sundial or a water clock, to understand that the hours are shown by art and not by chance, and then to suppose that the world, which embraces these very arts and their practitioners and everything else, is without design and reason.
And if someone carried into Scythia or Britain that globe which our friend Posidonius lately made, each single revolution of which produces the same effect in the sun and the moon and the five wandering stars
as is produced in the sky on each day and night, who among those barbarians would doubt that the globe was finished by reason?
Yet these men are in doubt about the world, from which everything arises and comes to be, whether it was made by chance or by some necessity, or by divine reason and mind.
They think Archimedes was better at imitating the revolutions of the sphere than nature was at producing them, especially when the real ones are finished far more skilfully than these imitations." This, as you hear, is what stands written in Cicero.
Having said this, he takes from the poet Accius that rough herdsman and turns him to his purpose: the man who from far off on a mountain catches sight of a ship he had never seen before, the ship in which the Argonauts were sailing to Colchis,
and, thunderstruck by the strangeness of the marvel and afraid, guesses at many things to himself: that a mountain or rock had been torn out of the bowels of the earth and was being driven over the sea by the winds, or that black whirlwinds had been massed together by the clash of the waves, or something of that kind;
and then, when he has seen the young men by whose strength and labour the ship was driven, and heard the sailors' song, and made out the faces of the heroes, comes to himself and lays aside his mistake and his amazement and begins to understand what the thing is.
Straight after this Cicero adds: "Therefore," he says, "as this man at first sight thinks he is looking at something lifeless and without feeling, but afterwards, from surer signs, begins to guess what it is that he had been in doubt about, so the philosophers ought,
if the first sight of the world happened to confuse them, afterwards, when they had seen its motions bounded and regular and everything governed by fixed order and unchanging constancy, to have understood that there is someone within,
not only as a dweller in this heavenly and divine house, but also as its ruler and director, and as the architect, so to speak, of so great a work and so great a charge."
