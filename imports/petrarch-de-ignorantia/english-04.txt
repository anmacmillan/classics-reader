The story as it stands is this. Those four friends come to me as usual: you do not need their names, since you know them all,
and the inviolable law of friendship does not allow friends to be named, however unfriendly a single act of theirs may be. They come two by two, as likeness of character or some chance throws them together.
Sometimes all of them at once. And they come with a remarkable sweetness, cheerful faces, pleasant talk. I should not doubt their good intentions, except that through some crack or other an unhappy spite has crept into souls that deserved a better lodger.
It is an incredible business, true though it is; and I could wish it were not so true.
The man they want not merely safe but happy, whom they not only love but court, visit and revere, to whom they take every pains to be not merely kind but attentive and generous — human nature,
how full you are of sicknesses, open and hidden! — that same man they envy. Envy him what? I do not know, I confess, and I am baffled when I look into it.
Not wealth, certainly, in which each of them outdoes me by as much as "the British whale is bigger than the dolphins", as the poet says; and besides, they wish me both greater wealth and moderate wealth, and they know mine is not kept to myself but shared, not haughty but very humble,
without boasting, without disdain, and altogether unworthy of any envy. Not my friends, of whom death has taken the greater part, and whom, like everything else, I gladly share with my friends.
Not bodily looks, which, if I ever had any, have faded before the years that conquer everything; and though by God's bounty and keeping my body still serves well enough for my age, it long ago ceased to be anything to envy — and even if it were still what it once was,
could I today, or could I then, have forgotten that line of the poet which I drank in as a small boy, "beauty is a brittle good", or that saying of Solomon in the book where he instructs a little child, "favour is deceitful and beauty is vain"?
How then should they envy me something I do not have, something I despised while I had it, and which, if it were given back now, I should despise all the more, having learned and tested how unstable it is?
Nor, finally, learning or eloquence. Of the first they insist I have none at all; the second, if I had any, is despised among them in the modern philosophical fashion and rejected as beneath a lettered man.
So now nothing is honoured but the babyish talk and tangled stammering of the philosophiser, a wisdom propped on a raised eyebrow and yawning, as Cicero calls it; and no one calls to mind Plato, the most eloquent of men, nor, to say nothing of the rest,
Aristotle, sweet and agreeable in himself, but made rough by these men.
So far do they desert or stray from their own leader that eloquence, which he thought a great ornament of philosophy and worked to join to it, stirred, they say, by the fame of the orator Isocrates, these men reckon a hindrance and a disgrace;
and last of all, not virtue itself, which is beyond doubt the best of things and the most envied, but cheap in their eyes, I think, because it is neither swollen nor showy.
This is what I should truly wish for myself, and it is exactly what they grant me, with one voice and gladly: having denied me small things, they hand over the greatest as though it were a trifling gift.
A good man they call me, indeed the best of men — I who, God grant, may not turn out bad, and not the worst of all, in the judgment of God.
The same man they pronounce wholly unlettered and an ignoramus, though the opposite was once settled by the judgment of learned men; how truly, I do not trouble myself. I set no great store by what is taken from me, provided that what is granted were true.
Most eagerly would I divide with these brothers of mine the inheritance of mother nature and of heavenly grace on these terms: let them all be lettered, and let me be good.
Of letters I would know either nothing, or only so much as serves for the daily praises of God. But alas, I fear that a humble prayer will fail me and a proud opinion will fail them.
They declare me mild, of good character and of great fidelity in friendship; and in this last point, unless I deceive myself, they are not deceived.
That, moreover, is why they count me among their friends: not any talent, not application, not learning, not devotion to the honourable arts, nor any hope of ever hearing and learning the truth from me.
So it has come round exactly to what Augustine tells of his own Ambrose: "I began to love him," he says, "not as a teacher of the truth, but as a man who was kind to me";
or to what Cicero thinks of Epicurus, whose character and spirit he approves in many places, while everywhere condemning his intellect and rejecting his teaching.
