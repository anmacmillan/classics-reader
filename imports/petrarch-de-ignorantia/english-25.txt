And who does not give it him, except the mad and noisy crowd of the schoolmen?
As for Averroes preferring Aristotle to everyone, that comes of his having taken on the exposition of his books and having in a way made them his own; and although they deserve much praise, the man praising them is suspect.
It comes back to the old proverb: every merchant is in the habit of praising his own wares.
There are men who dare write nothing of their own and who, greedy to write, become expositors of other men's works; and, like people who know nothing of architecture, make whitewashing walls their business and seek praise from it,
praise which they do not hope to win by themselves, nor through others unless they first praise those men and their books, the subject they have settled on, and praise it hotly and immoderately and always with much hyperbole.
How great the crowd is — shall I say of men expounding other men's work, or of men wrecking it? — especially in our own day, the book of the Sentences above all, which has suffered a thousand such workmen, would testify in a clear and complaining voice, if it could speak.
And what commentator ever failed to praise the work he had taken up as though it were his own? Indeed all the more lavishly, because to praise another's work is good manners, and to praise one's own is vanity and pride.
I pass over those who have picked out whole volumes for themselves, of whom Averroes is one, or the first.
Certainly Macrobius, who is not only an expositor but an outstanding writer, though he had picked out for exposition not the books of Cicero's Republic but a part of one book, is well known for what he added at the end of that exposition:
"It must truly be pronounced," he says, "that nothing is more perfect than this work, in which the whole completeness of philosophy is contained."
Imagine him speaking not about part of a book but about the entire books of all the philosophers: he might have used more words, but he could not have said more, since nothing can be added to completeness except what is superfluous.
And what more can be contained in all the books of the philosophers, written or still to be written, than the completeness of philosophy?
If, that is, this completeness ever could be or ever will be contained in all the books together, and if something is not missing from the first of them and will not be missing from the last.
But enough of this. I know, as I said, that I have run on a hard reef for my reputation, in not merely mentioning such great philosophers but setting up a comparison. Let my pen be excused by the ignorance charged against me and not denied by me, which is apt to make men bold and talkative.
The fear of losing glory or of diminishing one's name, which usually reins in speakers, has been taken from me by my friends' verdict. What am I to fear, I ask? What has been lost can no longer be lost or diminished for me.
Whatever I say will be either what my friends judge, or something more: there is nothing less than nothing.
So, since I have come out this far, driven by whatever wind, I shall get clear as best I can, and shall say what I remember often answering, sometimes to great questioners.
If the question is asked about Plato and Aristotle, which was the greater and more distinguished man, my ignorance is not so great, however much my judges assign me, that I would dare to rush out a verdict on so great a matter,
when a verdict must be held back and weighed even in small matters.
Nor does it escape me how great a contest has often arisen among the learned about learned men: about Cicero and Demosthenes, and likewise about Cicero and Virgil, and further about Virgil and Homer, about Sallust and Thucydides,
and finally about Plato himself and his fellow pupil Xenophon, and about many others. If the investigation of all these is difficult and the appraisal doubtful, who is to sit and pronounce with the authority of a judge between Plato and Aristotle?
But if the question is which of them is the more praised, I shall settle it without hesitation: the difference between them is, as I judge, the difference between two men of whom one is praised by rulers and nobles and the other by the whole populace.
Plato is praised by the greater men, Aristotle by the more numerous; and both deserve to be praised by the great and by the many, indeed by everyone. For in natural and human matters both reached the point that can be reached by mortal ability and study.
In divine matters Plato and the Platonists climbed higher, although neither of them could arrive where he was heading.
But, as I said, Plato came nearer; and no Christian, and above all no faithful reader of Augustine's books, would hesitate about that;
and the Greeks do not conceal it either, unlettered as they are today, calling Plato, in the footsteps of their forebears, divine, and Aristotle daemonic.
