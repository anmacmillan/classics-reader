There are besides many things which would hold down and check any anger, if there were any, by the remedy of examples. For was learning, or holiness, or any virtue at all, ever so outstanding as not to lie open to detractors?
On the contrary, as Livy says, "the greater the glory, the nearer the envy." And so it is.
For although envy is a sluggish evil and does not climb into high minds, but crawls on the ground in the manner of a viper, as Ovid says,
still it is her habit to go after the roots of lofty glory the more greedily and to pour her venom into famous names, exactly like certain underground worms which try tall trees with secret bites at the roots and a silent rot.
So spite often rages in silence; but sometimes it boils over in loud shouting, the passion of the mind breaking the silence.
The Homeric Iliad tells of Thersites, lame in the foot, twisted in the legs, humped in the shoulders, hollow in the chest, half-bald on the crown and scabby, publicly running down Agamemnon, king of the Greeks, and Achilles, the bravest of the Greeks,
and Virgil's Aeneid records Drances insulting Turnus with words. But there is nothing surprising in that, since hatred between opposites is natural. How much was said against the deified Julius and Augustus Caesar, whether by friends or by enemies.
What astonishes me past belief is that Pescennius Niger, a most brave man, used to say that the line of the Scipios, foremost in the whole Roman world, was lucky rather than brave. There was certainly no spite in this, but an ill-advised freedom of judgment.
But these and the like are foreign to us and far off; let us come to things nearer home. I could mention the saints, and Jerome above all; but my matter is secular and the talk is only of letters.
So I shall touch on those, but not all of them, who lie closer to my complaint.
First of all, then, who has not heard of Epicurus running everyone down, out of intolerable pride, or envy, or both: Pythagoras, Empedocles, Timocrates, whom, friend though he was, he is said to have torn apart in whole volumes,
because in philosophy he differed a little from him and from his mad opinions?
These three, though, and the others he tore at, have grounds for patience, since the same man remarkably despises Plato and abuses Aristotle most insultingly, and Democritus too, from whom, last of all, he had learned everything he knew in philosophy,
and whom he followed in everything with only a slight change of words: this man he defamed most sharply, since of course he boasted of having had no master and wished to be thought so.
Metrodorus and Hermarchus followed their teacher in this appetite for detraction, likewise tearing at the philosophers named above and sparing no man's greatness or glory.
Zeno too was abusive and a mocker, who, naming Chrysippus contemptuously, a most acute man and of his own sect, would always call him not Chrysippus but Chrysippa; and who assailed with insults and abuse not only his contemporaries
but Socrates, the father of philosophy, using a Latin word, so that the joke should bite harder in a foreign tongue, I suppose, and calling him repeatedly the Attic buffoon.
That very gibe — if it ought to be called a gibe and not rather an insult — was afterwards turned by rivals against Cicero himself, in whom these things stand written; for his remarkable wit of tongue some called him the consular buffoon:
a joke worthy not of that man's ears and character but rather of the mouths and buffoonery of those who made it.
Seneca's detraction of Quintilian and Quintilian's of Seneca are well known by now. Both were outstanding men, both Spaniards; yet they pick at each other with mutual bites, and each condemns the other's style. Astonishing indeed in such minds.
For the learned are usually an object of hatred and amazement to the unlearned; and so the unlearned gnaw at their reputation as soon as a chance shines out.
Among the learned, even when they do not know each other by sight, there is much kinship, unless spite or an appetite for pre-eminence has broken it off; which is credible enough in the case of the last two and of the others spoken of before.
And even when these have subsided, strange to say, a kind of rivalry seems to persist among distinguished men, like a sea still swelling when the winds have dropped; and I read in some writers of a double cause for it.
One is the partisanship of pupils and followers, which drags men who would rather be quiet into a contest by opposing verdicts;
the other is equality itself, which, without the awareness of the men brought into comparison, drives the judgments of onlookers different ways, so that two men in agreement with each other and free of passion may still seem to be silently contending between themselves,
like two neighbouring mountains, or two tall towers, about height and prominence. An example of this, unless my memory fails me, is the pair of men mentioned above, Plato and Xenophon.
