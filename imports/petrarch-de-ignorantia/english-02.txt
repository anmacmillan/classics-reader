Shall we then never rest? Will this pen always be at war? Shall we have no holidays? Must we answer every day to the praises of friends and every day to the wrangling of rivals? Will seclusion not shut envy out, nor time put it out?
Will my flight from nearly everything for which the human race labours and burns not have won me quiet? Will an age already declining and worn out not have brought me leisure at last? What a stubborn poison it is!
The age that would long since have excused me from public business does not yet excuse me from envy; and while the one to which I owe much releases me, the other, to which I owe nothing, torments me.
Once, I admit, it was the season for a friendlier pen, and a calmer manner of speech always suited my nature and now suits my years. Forgive me, friends; and you, reader, whoever you are, be indulgent, I beg.
And you before all others, best of men, Donato, to whom I say this, pardon me. I must speak, not because speaking is better, but because the opposite is hard. For though reason counsels silence, an indignation that is deserved, unless I am mistaken, and a just grief wring the words out of me.
Greedy as I am for peace, I am forced into war. Once again, look, I am pushed forward against my will; once again I am haled before a censor's tribunal, and the strange thing is that I cannot tell whether to call it an envious friendship or a friendly envy.
What can you not do, shameless spite, if you can set even friendly minds alight? I have known much, but this kind of evil I had not known until now, when my luck throws it in my way for the first time, the heaviest and the worst of all.
For encounters with enemies are often successful; anger is sweet, as some hold, and victory certainly is. But for a man fighting it out with friends, both winning and losing are wretched.
My war, though, is neither with friends only nor with enemies, but with envy. The enemy is not new, though her manner of fighting is unusual: she comes down into the line with her quiver on, attacks with arrows, strikes from far off.
She has this much good in her: she is blind, so that she is easily dodged if you see her coming, and, since she shoots without choosing, she often wounds her own. This is the monster I must now run through without harming friendship.
It is a delicate business, out of two locked in each other's arms, to stab the one and leave the other unhurt.
You remember, I think, how at Alexandria Caesar, surrounded by an unexpected battle, dragged king Ptolemy with him through every turn of the war, so as not to perish without him:
which was, it is believed, no small reason for his escape, since those who hated the one and loved the other judged it hard to kill the first and save the second at the same time.
Nor, I think, has this slipped your mind: on the day when the kingdom of Persia was freed from a slavish tyranny by the shrewdness of Otanes and the courage of seven brave men, Gobryas, one of the conspirators, grappled one of the two tyrants in a dark place
and urged his companions to strike the man even through his own body, in case by sparing him they let the other get away.
And so now sacred friendship cries out to me to strike wicked spite with the pen's point even through her own side, since she nurses it in her lap in an embrace not of equals. It is hard, in such darkness, to tell apart things so closely joined.
Still I shall try, so that as the enemy fell then while Gobryas came through unhurt, so now, with bitter envy refuted and destroyed, sweet friendship may be safe;
and if that friendship is true, for which true virtue is required, then, where it cannot be otherwise, it would rather be wounded with envy put out than go unwounded while envy survives and reigns over it.
