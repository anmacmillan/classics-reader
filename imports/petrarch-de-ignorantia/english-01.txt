Here at last, my friend, is the book you expected and I promised: a small book on an enormous subject, my own ignorance and that of many others. Had I been free to beat it out on the anvil of wit with the hammer of study, believe me, it would have swollen into a camel's load.
For what wider ground is there for speech, what vaster field, than a treatment of human ignorance, and above all of my own?
Read it as you are used to hearing me talk before the fire on winter nights, wandering wherever the impulse carries me.
It is called a book, but it is a conversation. It has nothing of a book about it but the name — not the bulk, not the arrangement, not the style, not, in the end, the weight, having been written in haste on a hurried journey.
I chose to call it a book so as to earn your favour with a small gift under a large name; and although I am confident that everything of mine pleases you, I meant even so to deceive you in this way.
There is such a practice of deception among friends: when we are about to send a handful of apples or a scrap of food, we shut it in a silver dish and wrap it in white linen. What is sent is no greater and no better, but it is more welcome to the receiver and more creditable to the sender.
So I too have dignified a small thing with a handsome covering, calling that a book which I might have called a letter; and it will be no cheaper in your eyes for being threaded through with erasures and additions and hemmed about with a margin full on every side.
For though something of its beauty is taken from the eye, as much charm should seem added for the mind, since from this above all you understand how intimate you are with me, that I should write to you in such a state,
so that you may look on the additions and erasures as so many tokens of intimacy and affection.
Nor, besides, could you doubt that it is mine, coming to you written in the hand you have long known so well and disfigured, as if on purpose, by so many scars; which reminds me that Suetonius Tranquillus wrote something of the kind about the emperor Nero:
"There came into my hands," he says, "notebooks and papers with some very well known verses, written in his own hand, so that it was easy to see they had not been copied out or taken down from someone dictating,
but plainly written as by a man thinking and composing, so many were the deletions and insertions and words written above the line." So much for him. I shall write you nothing further now. Live mindful of me, and farewell.
Padua, the Ides of January, on my sickbed, at the eleventh hour of the night.
