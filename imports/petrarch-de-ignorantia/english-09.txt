Certainly I have never read the story of Laberius without a kind of fellow-feeling, as though I foresaw what was in store for me: after spending his whole life in honourable service, at sixty he was at last, by the coaxing and the requests of Julius Caesar,
requests that come out armed from the mouths of princes, brought on to the stage, and from a Roman knight became a mime.
He did not bear the injury in silence, but complained at length, and among other things in these words: "I, after sixty years lived without a stain, went out of my house a Roman knight and shall return home a mime. Truly I have lived one day longer
than I should have lived." As for me — I may boast in your company — never truly lettered, but once believed to be so, I left my house as a boy and have not returned even as an old man, and have worn away almost my whole life in study.
Hardly a day ever passed idly while my health held, without my reading, or writing, or thinking about letters, or listening to others read, or questioning them when they fell silent.
And I went not only to learned men but to learned cities, so as to come back from them more learned and better: first to Montpellier, since in the years of my boyhood I was nearer that place, then to Bologna, afterwards to Toulouse, and to Paris and Padua,
and to Naples, where there then flourished — I know I am pricking many ears — Robert, greatest of the kings and philosophers of our age, whose glory in learning was not below his glory in ruling;
whom my judges call ignorant, so that I count a disgrace shared with so great a king almost a glory, though the two of us may share it with others still greater in reputation and in age, of whom I shall speak at the end;
for the rest, about this king the whole world, and the truth, held the opposite opinion. As a young man I revered that old man not as a king, since kings are everywhere and plentiful, but as a rare miracle of intellect and an awesome sanctuary of letters.
So far below him in fortune and in years, I was yet dear to him on the most intimate footing, as many still know, especially in that city; not through any merits of mine or of my family, nor through military or courtly accomplishments, of which I had absolutely none,
but, as he used to say, through my intellect and my letters. Either then he was a bad judge, or I have been a very bad keeper of the gift, having unlearned it by constant study and labour.
Besides, I spent the greatest and best part of my life on study in that court which for some reason they called Roman, on the left bank of the Rhône, where it passed fifty years or more, and from which lately, in this very year, never to return, I hope,
it departed under the leadership and auspices of Urban V, a saint if he holds to his course, and sought again the kindly city and the most sacred see of Peter, there to remain, I hope; and not far from that court, in my transalpine Helicon, where the Sorgue, king of springs, rises,
I used up that time. In the one place there was a constant gathering of nearly all the lettered men of our world; in the other, solitude and silence and a quiet perfectly suited to thought.
So there by studying, and going now to the schools and now to the masters, and now reciting to friends what I had learned or written; here by wandering and thinking, and, sinner though I am, often by praying too,
and always in my own company, and rarely talking of anything but liberal studies: all my time has been spent on letters.
In the meantime I came to be known and liked by a thousand learned and respected old men; and if I went on to count them, the recollection would be sweet, but the catalogue would turn out anything but short.
To all of them I was agreeable for this reason alone, or chiefly: that as a young man I had, in cities given to study, the reputation of a lettered man, the reputation which now, in a seafaring city, four young men take from an old one by a verdict.
