Things standing thus, one may well ask what it is they envy me, since that they envy something is not in doubt; for they dissemble badly and put no bridle on tongues driven by the goad within;
and in men otherwise well ordered and not without sense, what is that but a plain sign of an ungoverned passion? If they envy, as they do, and there is nothing else for them to envy, then the hidden poison lays itself open.
For they envy one empty thing only, such as it is: a name, and this reputation, which has fallen to a living man perhaps beyond his deserts and beyond the common practice, which very rarely celebrates the living.
On this they have fixed their sidelong eyes, a thing I could wish myself rid of now and often before; for I remember it doing me harm more often than good, and while it has made me not a few friends, it has made me enemies past counting, so that it has gone with me
as it goes with men who march into battle in a conspicuous helmet and with no great strength, for whom the glitter of the crest does nothing but get them struck by more hands.
This plague was thoroughly familiar to me once, in greener years, but it has never been more troublesome than in the form that has now flared up, since I am less fit than I was for entering on youthful wars and taking such burdens on my back,
while envy is born again where I neither deserve it nor feared it, and at a time when it ought to have been beaten by my way of life or worn out by age.
But let me go on. They think themselves great, and they are all plainly rich, which is the one greatness mortals now recognise. They feel, though many deceive themselves on the point, that they have won no name and, if they read the future rightly, can hope for none.
Among these anxieties they waste away, and — such is the force of the disease — like mad dogs they put out their tongues and sharpen their teeth even against friends, and wound those they love. What blindness is this? What madness?
Was it not so that a mother in her frenzy tore Pentheus apart, and Hercules his small sons?
These men love me and everything of mine except one thing, my name; and I do not refuse to change it. Let me be called Thersites or Choerilus, or whatever else they prefer, if only that way I may secure that so honourable a love has no exception in it at all.
They burn the more fiercely and blaze with a blind fire because they are all students themselves and great burners of midnight oil.
With this qualification: the first knows no letters at all — I am telling you nothing you do not know — the second few, the third not many, while the fourth knows not a few, I grant, but so tangled and so disordered, and, as Cicero says, with such frivolity and swagger,
that it might have been better to know none. For letters are, to many, instruments of madness and, to nearly all, of pride, unless, and this is rare, they happen to fall on a good and well-ordered soul.
So that fourth man has a great deal to say about beasts and birds and fishes: how many hairs the lion has on his head, how many feathers the hawk in his tail, in how many coils the octopus binds a drowning man; how elephants couple facing away and are pregnant for two years;
how the elephant is a teachable, long-lived creature, closest to human intelligence, and lives on to the end of a second or third century; how the phoenix is consumed in a spiced fire and, once burnt, is born again;
how the remora checks a prow driven at any speed, yet can do nothing once pulled out of the water; how the hunter tricks the tigress with a mirror, the Arimaspian attacks the griffin with steel, whales deceive the sailor with their backs;
how the bear's cub is born shapeless, the mule's offspring is rare, the viper's is single and fatal to its mother; how moles are blind and bees deaf; and how, last of all, the crocodile alone of living things moves its upper jaw.
Most of this is false, as has become clear with many such things once they were brought into our part of the world, or at any rate was never verified by the authors themselves, but, because the subject was far off, was believed the more readily or invented the more freely;
and finally, even if it were all true, it contributes nothing whatever to a happy life.
For what use is it, I ask, to know the natures of beasts and birds and fishes and snakes, and either not to know or to despise the nature of man: what we were born for, where we come from and where we are going?
These things and others like them I would often urge, more freely than they are used to hearing and perhaps less warily than I should, against these scribes, most learned not in the law of Moses or of Christ but, as they suppose, of Aristotle;
and since I was speaking among friends and foresaw no danger in it, they were first astonished, and then angry.
And since they took this as spoken against their sect and their ancestral laws, they too gathered a council, not to condemn me, whom they certainly love, but to condemn my reputation, which they hate, on a charge of ignorance.
If only they had summoned others as well: the verdict to be delivered might then have met with some contradiction in council. As it was, so that the sentence should be of one mind and one voice, the four met alone.
There they said much and various things about a man absent and undefended, not that they differed in mind, since they all thought one thing and were going to say one thing, but they argued against themselves and their own verdict, in the manner of expert judges,
so that, with the truth as it were strained and pressed out through the narrows of objection, they might pronounce with more colour.
