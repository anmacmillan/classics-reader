But what do you want? Or what do you suppose? You are waiting, I imagine, for the judges' sentence.
So, when everything had been weighed, with some god or other before their eyes — since there is no god who wills injustice, and no god of envy or of ignorance, which I should call the twin cloud over the truth — they delivered this short and final judgment:
that I am a good man without letters. If only they had never said, and will never say, anything true but this one thing.
And you, kind and saving Jesus, true God and giver of all letters and of ability, true king of glory and lord of the virtues: on the bended knees of my soul I beg you now that, if you will grant me no more, this at least may be my portion,
that I may be a good man, which I cannot be unless I love you greatly and worship you devoutly.
For this is what I was born to, not to letters; which, if they come alone, puff up and pull down instead of building: glittering chains, a laborious business, and a noisy weight upon the soul.
You know, Lord, before whom all my longing and all my sighing lies, that from letters, whenever I have used them soberly, I have sought nothing more than to become good.
Not that I trusted letters to do this, or anyone else at all, though Aristotle and many others promised as much: you alone can do it.
But I judged that through letters the road I was travelling would be more honourable, more certain and at the same time more pleasant, with you as my guide and no other. You know, I say, you who search the reins and the marrow, that it is as I say.
I was never so young, and never so greedy for glory, which at times I do not deny I was, as not to prefer being good to being learned.
I wanted both, I admit, since human desire is endless and cannot be filled until it comes to rest in you, above whom there is nothing for it to raise itself towards.
I wanted two things; but since one of them is snatched away or refused, I am grateful to my judges for leaving me the better of the two, provided they were not lying about that as well, and did not, in order to take from me what they wanted, hand me something that was not there.
That way I might console myself for my loss, but with an empty consolation, they having followed with me the habit of women's envy, which, if it is complaining about a neighbour's beauty, calls her good and well behaved and concedes her every title, false though they be,
so as to take away the one that is perhaps true: the name of beautiful.
But you, my God, lord of the sciences, outside whom there is no other, whom I ought and wish to set above Aristotle and above any philosophers and poets you please, and above all who multiply words, glorying in their lofty talk,
and above letters and learning and, in short, above everything: you can grant me truly the name of a good man, which they grant me falsely; and I pray that you will.
And I ask not so much for a good name, which Solomon prefers to precious ointments, as for the thing itself: that I may be good, that I may love you and deserve to be loved by you — for no one repays those who love him as you do — that I may think of you, obey you, hope in you,
and speak of you. Let the old things depart from my mouth, and let my thoughts be made ready for you.
For truly the bow of the mighty has been overcome and the weak have been girded with strength; and one of those little ones who believe in you is far happier than Plato, than Aristotle, than Varro, than Cicero,
who with all their letters did not know you; and brought up against you and joined to you, who are the rock, their judges were swallowed up, and a lettered ignorance was laid bare.
So let letters belong to these men who take them from me; or, since they cannot belong to them, unless I am mistaken, let them belong to whoever can have them.
Let these men keep their huge opinion of their own affairs, and the bare name of Aristotle, which in its five syllables delights so many ignorant people;
and, besides, an empty joy, and a self-exaltation with no foundation and close to collapse, and all the fruit that the ignorant and puffed-up gather from their errors with a loose and easy credulity.
Let mine be humility, and the knowledge of my own ignorance and frailty, and contempt for nothing but the world and myself and the insolence of those who despise me; mistrust of myself, hope in you;
and last, God for my portion, and that unlettered virtue which they do not envy me. They will certainly laugh if they hear this, and say that I talk piously and without letters, like any little old woman.
For to these men, swollen with the stamp of letters, nothing is cheaper than piety, than which nothing is dearer to the truly wise and soberly lettered, for whom it is written:
"Piety is wisdom". And by my words they will be confirmed more and more in their verdict that I am good without letters.
