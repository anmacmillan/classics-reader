I come back to my censors, about whom I have said much and, so that nothing may be hidden from you, must say something more. For I should not like to be called mad and stupid as well as unlettered.
Letters are ornaments that come from outside; reason is inborn and part of the man himself. So it is not with reason as with letters: I should indeed be ashamed to have gone without it. And in fact it was not wanting, by which I might have sidestepped their snares.
I could not easily have been taken in by their arts; it was by my own openness that I was wrapped up, and by the most honourable veil of what I took to be faithful friendship. It is very easy to deceive a man who trusts. I have said it and I say it again.
Citizens of that most beautiful and greatest of cities used to come to see me, as many others did, usually two at a time and sometimes all together.
And I would be delighted, and receive them as though they were so many angels of God, forgetting everything but them, who filled my whole mind and cleared it wonderfully. At once, as among friends, there was much and varied talk.
I took no care how or what I said, or about anything else, except that my face should be glad and my mind gladder at the coming of such guests, so that sometimes joy forced me into silence, and sometimes a kind of deference, for fear of getting in the way
of men eager to talk, as happens when everyone speaks at once; and so I said now nothing and now commonplaces.
For in friendship I have learned to dress nothing up, to hide nothing, to feign nothing, but to keep my mind on my tongue and on my face, and to say everything to my friends as I say it to myself, than which, as Cicero says, nothing is sweeter.
For what need is there to parade eloquence or learning before friends, who see your mind, your feelings, your very intelligence? Unless perhaps they ask something not to test you but to learn.
And even then no display or ornament is called for, but a faithful sharing of knowledge, as of everything else, without reservation and without envy.
So I often wonder that Caesar Augustus, so great a ruler, could take on so much care over so small a matter among all his other cares about the greatest affairs, that not only to the people or the senate or the soldiers,
but even with his wife and his friends he would say nothing except deliberately and often from a written text. He did this, perhaps, so that nothing superfluous or silly should slip out of him by chance, by which that heavenly discourse might be criticised or despised.
Let that be allowed to him, addressing his subjects from the highest summit in writings that served as oracles. My own talk among friends wanders, and my sentences are unpolished.
Farewell to eloquence, if it has to be sought with such unbroken effort. I would rather be inarticulate than always anxious and gloomy.
I have always kept to this rule among those dear and close to me, and especially among those who knew what I could do; and lately I kept to it above all among these friends of ours, and so, through a friendly confidence, I fell unawares into a hostile slander.
For nothing came out carefully or anxiously: whatever came first into my mind and to my mouth came out first.
They, gathered round by arrangement, weighed every word, and took whatever I said as though I could have said nothing better and nothing more polished than that.
Having done this once and then again and again, they were easily confirmed in the verdict they wanted to be true. Nothing, of course, is easier than persuading people who are willing and already believe.
So they spoke to me the more confidently, as to an ignorant man, and, I believe, they also laughed at my want of knowledge, which at the time I never suspected. So one incautious man, ambushed by the plots of several, am I, an ignoramus, mixed in with the herds of the ignorant.
