They used to throw out for discussion either an Aristotelian problem or something about animals.
And I would keep quiet, or make a joke, or start on something else, and sometimes ask with a smile how Aristotle could possibly have known a thing for which there was no argument and no possible experiment.
They would be astounded, and silently angry, and look at me as at a blasphemer, because I looked for something other than that man's authority to settle a question,
so that we have plainly turned from philosophers and devoted lovers of wisdom into Aristotelians, or rather Pythagoreans, reviving that ridiculous custom by which it was not permitted to ask anything except whether the master had said it.
And the master was Pythagoras, as Cicero says.
For my part I hold that Aristotle was a great man and knew many things, but that he was a man, and therefore could be ignorant of some things, indeed of many. I shall say more, if these men allow it, who are friends less of the truth than of their sects:
I believe, by heaven, and I do not doubt, that he went wrong by the whole road, as they say, not only in small matters, where the error is small and not at all dangerous, but in the greatest, which bear on the sum of our salvation.
And although he discussed happiness at length at the beginning and the end of the Ethics, I shall dare to say — let my censors shout as they please — that he was so wholly ignorant of true happiness that in the knowledge of it, I will not say more subtle,
but happier, was any devout old woman, or a faithful fisherman or shepherd, or a farmer.
So I wonder the more that some of our own men have admired that Aristotelian treatise so far as to reckon it a sacrilege, and to say so in writing, for anyone to speak about happiness after him; whereas to me — I may be saying this boldly, but,
unless I am mistaken, truly — he seems to have seen happiness as the owl sees the sun: that is, its light and its rays, but not the thing itself;
since he founded it not within its own borders nor on solid ground, but like a lofty building far off in enemy country and on shaky foundations, and did not understand, or, having understood, disregarded,
those things without which happiness simply cannot exist, namely faith and immortality; and I already regret having said that he either failed to understand these or disregarded them, for I ought to have said only the first.
They were not understood; he did not know them, and could not have known them or hoped for them, since the true light had not yet shone on the earth, the light that lightens every man coming into this world.
He and the rest fashioned for themselves what they wanted, what all men naturally want and no one can want the opposite of: happiness, I mean, which they decked out in words and sang of like an absent mistress without ever seeing her, and rejoiced over nothing,
happy exactly as men are happy in a dream, but wretched in truth, and destined to be woken into their wretchedness by the thunder of a death close at hand, and then with open eyes to see what that happiness was which they had discussed in their sleep.
And so that no one may think all this is said out of my own head and therefore said too rashly, let him read the thirteenth book of Augustine's On the Trinity, where on this very point, and against the philosophers who made for themselves, to use his word, each as it pleased him,
their own happy lives, he will find much argued weightily and sharply. I admit I have said this often, and I shall say it as long as I can speak, because I am confident that I have spoken and shall speak the truth.
If they think this sacrilege, let them charge me with violating religion, and Jerome with me, who cared not what Aristotle says but what Christ says.
I, for my part, should not hesitate to call them impious and sacrilegious if they think otherwise; and God will take my life and everything I hold dear from me sooner than this belief, which is devout, true and saving, or sooner than I deny Christ out of love for Aristotle.
By all means let them be philosophers, let them be Aristotelians, though beyond doubt they are neither; but let them be both. I do not grudge them these bright names, on which they swell even when the names are false. Let them not grudge me the humble and true name of Christian and Catholic.
But why do I ask for what I know they do and will go on doing of their own accord? They do not in fact grudge us these names: they despise them as simple and low, beneath their talents and unworthy of them.
