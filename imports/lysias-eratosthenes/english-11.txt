Now read me this law as well, the one from the pillar on the Areopagus.
Law.
You hear, gentlemen, that the court of the Areopagus itself, which by ancestral right and in our own day too has the task of trying cases of homicide, is expressly told not to convict of murder a man who catches an adulterer with his wife and takes this vengeance.
And the lawgiver was so convinced that this was just in the case of wedded wives that he imposed the same penalty even for concubines, who are worth less.
Yet clearly, if he had had any heavier penalty than this for the case of wives, he would have imposed it.
As it is, being unable to find anything stronger for them, he decided it should be the same for concubines too.
Now read me this law too.
Law.
You hear, gentlemen, that it lays down that if anyone shames a free man or boy by force, he must pay double damages;
but if it is a woman, one of those for whom killing is permitted, he is liable to the same penalty.
So, gentlemen, the lawgiver thought men who use force deserve a lighter penalty than those who seduce.
For the seducers he decreed death, for the others he doubled the damages, because he thought that those who get their way by force are hated by their victims, while seducers corrupt their victims' minds so thoroughly that they make other men's wives more attached to themselves than to their husbands, the whole household falls into their hands, and it becomes uncertain whose children the children are, the husbands' or the adulterers'.
That is why the man who made the law set death as their penalty.
