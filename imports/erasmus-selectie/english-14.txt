Now, I don't care a straw for those wise men who declare it most foolish and most arrogant if anyone sings his own praises.
Let it be as foolish as they like, so long as they admit it is fitting.
For what could be more apt than that Moria herself should trumpet her own praises and play her own flute?
For who could portray me better than I myself?
Unless perhaps someone knows me better than I know myself.
And yet I consider this in any case a good deal more modest than what the common run of nobles and wise men habitually do, who, out of a kind of perverse modesty, usually suborn some flattering orator or windbag poet, and hire him for a fee to hear their own praises from him, that is, pure lies;
and yet all the while that bashful fellow spreads his feathers like a peacock and raises his crest, while the shameless flatterer makes a worthless man equal to the gods, while he holds him up as the perfect model of all virtues, when the man knows he is more than two whole octaves away from it: while he dresses the crow in borrowed plumes, while he washes the Ethiopian white, and finally while he makes an elephant out of a mouse.
Finally, I follow that well-worn proverb of the common people, which says that a man does right to praise himself if he has found no one else to praise him.
Still, I am meanwhile amazed at the ingratitude, or should I say the laziness, of mortals: although all of them eagerly worship me and gladly feel my kindness, yet in all these centuries no one has come forward to celebrate the praises of Folly in a grateful speech, when there has been no lack of people who have extolled Busirises, Phalarises, quartan fevers, flies, baldness and plagues of that kind in painstaking eulogies, worked out by lamplight at great cost of both oil and sleep.
From me you will hear a speech that is indeed improvised and unpolished, but all the truer for that.
