Especially since there are sometimes those who get through twice as much in wine as they pay for the meal. But before I finish with this meal, it is amazing to tell what a din and uproar of voices there is, once they have all begun to warm up with drink. In short? Everyone is deafened.
Sham fools often join in; and though no kind of man is more detestable, you would hardly believe how much the Germans delight in them: with their singing, chattering, shouting, dancing and stamping they make the stove-room seem about to collapse, and nobody can hear anyone else speak.
But meanwhile they think they are living delightfully: and there you have to sit, like it or not, until late at night.
Now at last finish the meal. For I too am tired of one so long.
I will.
At last, when the cheese has been removed, which they hardly like unless it is rotten and crawling with maggots, out comes that bearded man, bringing with him a trencher on which he has drawn some circles and half-circles in chalk: he puts it down on the table, silent and gloomy all the while; you would say he was some Charon.
Those who recognise the drawing put down their money, then another, and another, until the plate is filled. Then, noting who has paid, he silently reckons it up: if nothing is missing, he nods.
What if there is something over?
Perhaps he would give it back, and they do so sometimes.
Does nobody protest against so unfair a reckoning?
Nobody with any sense. For he would at once hear: “What sort of man are you? You'll pay no more than the others.”
It is a free sort of people you describe.
And if anyone tired from the journey wants to go to bed straight after dinner, he is told to wait until the others go to bed too.
I seem to see Plato's republic.
Then each is shown his nest, and truly nothing but a bedroom: for there are only beds there, and nothing else besides that you could use or steal.
Is it clean there?
The same as at dinner; linen washed perhaps six months ago.
What happens to the horses meanwhile?
They are treated under the same discipline as the men.
But is the treatment the same everywhere?
In some places it is more civil, in some harsher than I have described: but in general it is like that.
What if I now told you how guests are treated in that part of Italy they call Lombardy; then in Spain; then in England and in Wales? For the English keep partly French, partly German customs, being a mixture of these two nations. The Welsh proclaim themselves the original, native English.
Please do tell. For I have never had the chance to go there.
At present I have no time. For the boatman told me to be there at three o'clock, unless I wanted to be left behind, and he has my bag: another opportunity will come for chattering to our hearts' content.
