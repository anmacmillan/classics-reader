While we were still rolling about beside the ship, which the waves swung this way and that at their will, the tiller struck and broke the thigh of the man holding the left-hand end.
So he was torn away; the priest prayed for eternal rest for him and took his place, urging me to hold on to my end bravely and kick my feet hard. Meanwhile we were drinking a great deal of salt water.
So Neptune had mixed us not just a salt bath but a salt drink as well: though the priest showed a remedy for that.
What, please?
Every time a wave came at us, he turned the back of his head to it, with his mouth shut.
That's a sturdy old man you're telling me about.
When we had swum like this for some time and made a little headway, the priest, since he was remarkably tall, said: “Take heart; I can feel the bottom.” I did not dare hope for such good luck: “We're too far from the shore,” I said, “to hope for the bottom.”
“No,” he said, “I can feel ground with my feet.” “It's perhaps one of the chests,” I said, “that the sea has rolled here.” “No,” he said, “I can clearly feel ground by scratching with my toes.”
When we had swum on for some while and he felt the bottom again, he said: “You do whatever seems best to you; I leave you the whole mast and trust myself to the shallows”; and at once, waiting for the waves to draw back, he went after them on foot as fast as he could run.
When the waves came in again, he clasped both knees with both hands and braced himself against the wave, hiding under the water the way divers and ducks do: as the wave went out again he popped up and ran. Seeing that this worked for him, I copied him.
On the sand stood men who braced themselves against the force of the waves by holding very long poles out to one another: strong men, used to the waves, so that the last of them held out his pole to whoever was swimming in. Once he had grabbed it, they all drew back towards the shore and he was hauled safely onto dry land.
In this way several were saved.
How many?
Seven: but two of these passed out from the warmth when they were brought to the fire.
How many of you were there on the ship?
Fifty-eight.
O cruel sea! It might at least have been content with a tenth, which is enough for the priests. From so great a number it gave back so few!
There we experienced the unbelievable kindness of the people, who supplied us with everything with wonderful eagerness: lodging, fire, food, clothes, money for the journey.
What people was it?
The Hollanders.
Nothing is kinder than that people, though it is surrounded by savage nations. You won't be visiting Neptune again after this, I suppose.
No, not unless God takes away my right mind.
And I would rather hear such stories than live them.
