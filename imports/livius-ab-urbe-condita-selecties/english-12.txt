So dreadful a deed stirred no less commotion of feeling at Rome than it had at Collatia; so from every part of the city people ran to the forum.
As soon as they had gathered there, the herald summoned the people before the commander of the Celeres (the royal cavalry), an office Brutus happened to hold at that time.
There he delivered a speech by no means in keeping with the mind and character he had feigned up to that day, about the violence and lust of Sextus Tarquinius, the unspeakable rape of Lucretia and her pitiful death, and the bereavement of Tricipitinus, for whom the cause of his daughter’s death was more outrageous and more pitiful than the death itself.
He added the arrogance of the king himself and the miseries and toils of the plebs, sunk in the work of digging out ditches and sewers; Roman men, conquerors of all the peoples around, had been turned from warriors into labourers and stonecutters.
He recalled the shameful murder of King Servius Tullius and the daughter who drove her accursed carriage over her father’s body, and he called on the gods who avenge parents.
By recounting these and, I believe, other still more dreadful things, which the outrage of the moment suggests but which are by no means easy for writers to relate, he drove the inflamed multitude to strip the king of his power and to decree the exile of Lucius Tarquinius with his wife and children.
He himself enlisted and armed the younger men, who were volunteering their names of their own accord, and set out for the camp at Ardea to stir up the army there against the king: he left the command in the city to Lucretius, who had already been appointed prefect of the city by the king.
In the midst of this uproar Tullia fled from her house, while men and women, wherever she went, cursed her and called on the Furies who avenge parents.
