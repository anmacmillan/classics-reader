While the others were given over to grief, Brutus, holding before him the knife he had drawn from Lucretia’s wound, still dripping with blood, said: “By this blood, most chaste until the prince’s outrage, I swear, and I call you, gods, to witness, that I will pursue Lucius Tarquinius Superbus, together with his criminal wife and his whole brood of children, with sword, with fire, with whatever force I can henceforth command, and that I will not allow them or anyone else to be king at Rome.”
Then he handed the knife to Collatinus, and after him to Lucretius and Valerius, who were astounded at the wonder of it, wondering where this new spirit in Brutus’ breast had come from.
They swore as they had been bidden; and, turned wholly from grief to anger, they followed Brutus as their leader, who from that moment was calling them to overthrow the monarchy.
They carried Lucretia’s body out of the house and brought it down to the forum, and, as happens, drew people together by the wonder of this strange event and by its outrage.
Each for himself complained of the king’s crime and violence.
They were moved both by the father’s grief and by Brutus, who rebuked their tears and idle complaints and urged them to do what befitted men, what befitted Romans: take up arms against those who had dared to act as enemies.
All the boldest of the young men came forward armed as volunteers; the rest of the youth followed too.
Then, leaving Lucretia’s father to hold Collatia and posting guards so that no one could report the rising to the royal family, the rest set out armed for Rome under Brutus’ leadership.
When they arrived there, wherever the armed crowd went it caused fear and uproar; yet when people saw the leading men of the state marching at its head, they reckoned that, whatever it was, it was not without good reason.
