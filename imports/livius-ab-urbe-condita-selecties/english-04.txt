By some chance sent from heaven the Tiber had overflowed its banks in sluggish pools, and nowhere could one get to the course of the river proper, and this gave the men carrying the infants hope that they could be drowned even in slack water.
So, as if they had carried out the king's command, they leave the boys in the nearest floodwater, where the Ruminal fig tree now stands (they say it used to be called the Romular fig).
In those days there were vast wildernesses in these parts.
The story goes that when the shallow water had left the floating basket, in which the boys had been exposed, on dry ground, a thirsty she-wolf coming down from the surrounding hills turned her course towards the crying of the children; that she offered them her teats, lowering them for the infants, so gently that the master of the royal herds found her licking the boys with her tongue (they say his name was Faustulus), and that he took them to his farmstead and gave them to his wife Larentia to be brought up.
There are some who think that Larentia, because she sold her body, was called a "she-wolf" among the shepherds, and that this gave rise to the marvellous tale.
Born in this way and raised in this way, as soon as they came of age they were not idle either at the farmsteads or with the herds, but roamed the woodland pastures hunting.
From this their bodies and spirits grew strong, and they no longer only stood up to wild beasts but made attacks on robbers laden with booty, shared out the plunder among the shepherds, and with them, as their band of young men grew day by day, took part in both serious business and games.
