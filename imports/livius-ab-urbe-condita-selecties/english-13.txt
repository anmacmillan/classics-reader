When news of these events reached the camp, the king, alarmed at this turn of affairs, set out for Rome to put down the rising, while Brutus changed his route — for he had learned of the king’s approach — so as not to meet him; and at about the same time, by different roads, Brutus reached Ardea and Tarquin Rome.
The gates were shut against Tarquin and exile was pronounced on him: the camp joyfully welcomed the liberator of the city, and the king’s sons were driven out of it.
Two of them followed their father and went into exile at Caere among the Etruscans.
Sextus Tarquinius, who had gone to Gabii as if to his own kingdom, was killed by those avenging old feuds which he himself had stirred up against himself by his murders and plundering.
Lucius Tarquinius Superbus reigned for twenty-five years.
Rome was ruled by kings from the founding of the city to its liberation for two hundred and forty-four years.
Then two consuls were elected at the centuriate assembly by the prefect of the city, according to the regulations of Servius Tullius: Lucius Junius Brutus and Lucius Tarquinius Collatinus.
