Spurius Lucretius came with Publius Valerius, son of Volesus, and Collatinus with Lucius Junius Brutus, with whom he happened to be returning to Rome when his wife’s messenger met him.
They found Lucretia sitting in grief in her bedroom.
At the arrival of her family tears welled up, and when her husband asked, “Is all well?”
“Far from it,” she said; “for what can be well with a woman who has lost her chastity?
The traces of another man, Collatinus, are in your bed; yet only the body has been violated, the mind is guiltless; death will be my witness.
But give me your right hands and your word that the adulterer shall not go unpunished.
It is Sextus Tarquinius who, an enemy in the guise of a guest, last night by force and under arms carried off from here a pleasure that will be deadly to me and, if you are men, to him.”
They all gave their word in turn; they comforted her, sick at heart, by turning the guilt from her who had been forced onto the author of the crime: it is the mind that sins, not the body, and where there has been no intention there is no guilt.
“It is for you,” she said, “to see what he deserves: as for me, though I acquit myself of wrongdoing, I do not free myself from punishment; nor shall any unchaste woman hereafter live by the example of Lucretia.”
She drove into her heart a knife that she had been keeping hidden under her clothes, and slumping forward over the wound she fell, dying.
