The traditions about the time before the city was founded or was about to be founded, which are adorned more by poetic tales than by reliable records of events, I intend neither to affirm nor to refute.
Antiquity is granted this indulgence, that by mingling the human with the divine it makes the beginnings of cities more august; and if any people ought to be allowed to hallow its origins and to trace them back to the gods as their founders, such is the Roman people's glory in war that, when it claims Mars above all as its own father and the father of its founder, the nations of mankind should accept this with the same calm as they accept its rule.
But however these and similar matters are regarded or judged, I for my part shall not set great store by them:
Rather, let each reader, I ask, turn his mind keenly to these questions: what life and what morals there were, through what men and by what skills, at home and in war, the empire was both won and enlarged; then let him follow in his mind how, as discipline slowly gave way, morals at first sank, as it were, then slid more and more, and then began to go headlong, until we have come to these times in which we can bear neither our vices nor their remedies.
This above all is what makes the study of history healthy and fruitful.
You behold lessons of every kind of example set out on a conspicuous monument; from there you may take for yourself and for your state what to imitate, and from there what to avoid, shameful in its beginning and shameful in its end.
For the rest, either love of the task I have undertaken deceives me, or no state was ever greater, or more upright, or richer in good examples, nor one into which greed and luxury made their way so late, nor where such great and lasting honour was paid to poverty and thrift.
So true is it that the less wealth there was, the less desire there was: lately riches have brought in greed, and abundant pleasures a longing to ruin ourselves and to destroy everything through excess and self-indulgence.
But let complaints, which will not be welcome even then when perhaps they will be necessary, at least be absent from the beginning of so great an undertaking:
We would rather begin with good omens and with vows and prayers to the gods and goddesses, if we too had the custom that poets have, so that they might grant a happy outcome to the start of so great a work.
