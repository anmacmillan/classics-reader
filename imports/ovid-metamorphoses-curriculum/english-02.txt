I asked whether it might be an altar of Naiads or of Faunus,
or of a native god, when my host replied as follows:
“No mountain divinity, young man, has this altar;
she calls it her own to whom once the royal wife
denied the world, whom wandering Delos scarcely
received when she prayed, at the time when the light island floated.
There, leaning on a palm beside Pallas’ tree,
Latona bore the twins, despite their stepmother.
From here too, after giving birth, she is said to have fled Juno
and to have carried at her breast her children, two divinities.
And now, when the oppressive sun scorched the fields,
the goddess, weary from her long toil, in the territory
of Chimaera-bearing Lycia, caught by thirst from the starry heat,
and her eager children had drained her milk-filled breasts.
By chance she saw a pool of moderate depth in the bottom
of a valley; there country people were gathering bushy
osiers together with rushes and the marsh-loving sedge.
She approached and, setting one knee down, the Titan’s daughter
pressed the ground, so that she might drink the cool water.
