Virtue, then, being of two kinds, the one intellectual and the other moral, the intellectual for the most part has both its origin and its growth from teaching, for which reason it needs experience and time, while the moral comes about from habit, whence it has also got its name by a small deviation from the word for habit (ethos).
From this it is also clear that none of the moral virtues arises in us by nature;
for nothing that exists by nature is habituated to be otherwise — for example, the stone, which by nature moves downward, could not be habituated to move upward, not even if someone were to habituate it by throwing it upward ten thousand times, nor could fire be habituated to move downward, nor could anything else that is naturally constituted one way be habituated to be another way.
Neither by nature, then, nor contrary to nature do the virtues arise in us, but we are naturally fitted to receive them, and are perfected through habit.
Further, in the case of whatever comes to us by nature, we first acquire the capacities and later exhibit the activities (which is clear in the case of the senses;
for we did not get the senses from seeing often or hearing often, but the reverse — having them we used them, we did not get them by using them);
but the virtues we acquire by first exercising them, just as in the case of the other arts as well;
for the things we must learn before doing them, these we learn by doing them — for example, men become builders by building and lyre-players by playing the lyre;
