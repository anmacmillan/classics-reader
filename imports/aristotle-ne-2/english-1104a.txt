But let this be agreed beforehand, that every account concerning things to be done ought to be given in outline and not with precision, just as we said at the beginning also that accounts must be demanded according to the subject matter;
and the things involved in actions and the things that are advantageous have nothing fixed, just as neither do the things concerning health.
And since the general account is of this kind, still more does the account concerning particulars lack precision;
for it falls under no art nor under any set of precepts, but the agents themselves must always consider what suits the occasion, just as is the case also in medicine and in navigation.
But although the present account is of this kind, we must try to give help.
First, then, this must be observed, that such things are of a nature to be destroyed by deficiency and excess (for we must use manifest evidences on behalf of things unseen), just as we see in the case of strength and health;
for both excessive exercises and deficient ones destroy strength, and likewise drink and food, when they become too much or too little, destroy health, while the proportionate amounts both produce and increase and preserve it.
So, then, it is also with temperance and courage and the other virtues.
For the man who flees and fears everything and endures nothing becomes a coward, and the man who fears nothing at all but goes toward everything becomes rash;
and likewise the man who enjoys every pleasure and abstains from none is licentious, while the man who flees every one, as the boors do, is a sort of insensible person;
so temperance and courage are destroyed by excess and deficiency, but preserved by the mean.
But not only do the comings-to-be and growths and destructions arise from the same things and by the same things, but the activities also will be in the same things;
for so it is also in the case of the other, more manifest things — for example, in the case of strength;
for it comes into being from taking much nourishment and enduring many exertions, and it is the strong man who would most be able to do these things.
And so it is also with the virtues;
for from abstaining from pleasures we become temperate, and having become so we are most able to abstain from them;
