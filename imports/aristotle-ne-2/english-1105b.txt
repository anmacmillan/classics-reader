But these conditions are not counted in toward the possession of the other arts, except the knowing itself;
whereas toward the possession of the virtues the knowing has little or no force, while the other conditions have not little but all the power — the very ones that come about from often doing just and temperate things.
Then things done are called just and temperate when they are such as the just man or the temperate man would do;
but a man is not just and temperate because he does these things, but only when he also does them in the way in which the just and the temperate do them.
It is well said, then, that from doing just things the just man comes to be, and from doing temperate things the temperate man;
and from not doing these things no one would have even a prospect of becoming good.
But the many do not do these things; taking refuge in argument instead, they think they are philosophizing and that in this way they will become serious men — doing something like the sick, who listen to their doctors attentively but do none of the things prescribed.
Then just as those men will not have their body in good condition by being treated in this way, neither will these have their soul in good condition by philosophizing in this way.
After these matters, we must examine what virtue is.
Since, then, the things that come to be in the soul are three — passions, capacities, states — virtue would be one of these.
By passions I mean appetite, anger, fear, confidence, envy, joy, friendly feeling, hatred, longing, emulation, pity — in general, the things on which pleasure or pain follows;
by capacities, the things in virtue of which we are said to be susceptible of these passions — for example, those in virtue of which we are able to be angered or pained or moved to pity;
and by states, the things in virtue of which we stand well or badly toward the passions — for example, toward being angered: if violently or slackly, we stand badly, but if in a middle way, well;
and similarly toward the others as well.
The virtues, then, are not passions, nor are the vices, because we are not called serious or base according to our passions but we are so called according to our virtues and vices, and because according to our passions we are neither praised nor blamed (for the man who fears is not praised, nor the man who is angered, nor is a man blamed for being angered simply, but for being angered in a certain way),
