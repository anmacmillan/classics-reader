Like a philosopher, Theophrastus says in his comparison of wrongdoings — as one might compare such things in a rather everyday way — that the offences committed through desire are graver than those committed through anger.
For the man who is angered appears to turn away from reason with a certain pain and an unnoticed contraction;
but he who sins through desire, being overcome by pleasure, appears somehow more intemperate and more effeminate in his wrongdoings.
Rightly then, and in a manner worthy of philosophy, he said that the sin committed with pleasure lies under a greater charge than the one committed with pain;
and in general the one seems rather like a man first wronged and compelled by pain to grow angry,
while the other has set out toward wrongdoing of his own motion, carried toward doing something by desire.
