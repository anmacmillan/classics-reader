Tell me, Muse, of the man of many turns, who very far and wide
was driven to wander, after he had sacked the sacred citadel of Troy;
and he saw the cities of many men and came to know their mind,
and many the woes he suffered on the sea in his heart,
striving to win his own life and the homecoming of his companions.
But not even so did he save his companions, though he longed to;
for by their own recklessness they perished,
fools, who the cattle of Hyperion the Sun
devoured; and he took from them the day of their homecoming.
Of these things, from some point at least, goddess, daughter of Zeus, tell us also.
Then all the others, as many as had escaped sheer destruction,
were at home, having escaped both the war and the sea;
but him alone, longing for his homecoming and his wife,
the queenly nymph Calypso, shining among goddesses, kept back
in her hollow caves, yearning that he be her husband.
But when at last the year came, as the seasons rolled round,
in which the gods had spun for him to return home
to Ithaca, not even there was he free of trials,
even among his own dear people. And all the gods pitied him
except Poseidon; he raged unceasingly
against godlike Odysseus, until he should reach his own land.
But he had gone among the Ethiopians who dwell far off,
the Ethiopians who are divided in two, the remotest of men,
some where Hyperion sets, the others where he rises,
to receive a hecatomb of bulls and rams.
There he sat and took his pleasure at the feast; but the others
were gathered together in the halls of Olympian Zeus.
And among them the father of men and gods began to speak;
for he remembered in his heart blameless Aegisthus,
whom far-famed Orestes, Agamemnon's son, had slain;
remembering him, he spoke these words among the immortals:
"Ah me, how indeed mortals now blame the gods!
For from us, they say, come their evils, yet they themselves
by their own recklessness have sorrows beyond what is fated,
as even now Aegisthus, beyond what was fated, the son of Atreus's
wedded wife has married, and slew the man himself on his return,
though he knew it was sheer destruction, since we told him beforehand,
sending Hermes, the keen-sighted slayer of Argus,
neither to kill the man nor to woo his wife;
for from Orestes will come vengeance for the son of Atreus,
whenever he comes of age and longs for his own land.
So spoke Hermes, but the mind of Aegisthus
he did not persuade, though meaning well; and now he has paid for it all at once."
Then the goddess, grey-eyed Athena, answered him:
"Our father, son of Cronos, highest of rulers,
truly that man lies low in a destruction well deserved;
so too may any other perish who does such deeds;
but my heart is torn for war-minded Odysseus,
the ill-fated one, who far from his dear ones has long been suffering woes
on a sea-girt island, where the navel of the sea lies.
a wooded island, and a goddess dwells there in her halls,
the daughter of baneful-minded Atlas, who of the whole sea
knows the depths, and himself holds the pillars,
tall ones, which keep earth and heaven apart.
His daughter detains that wretched, grieving man,
and ever with soft and wheedling words
she charms him, so that he may forget Ithaca; but Odysseus,
longing to catch sight of even the smoke leaping up
from his own land, yearns to die. And not even so is your
own heart moved, Olympian. Did not Odysseus
beside the ships of the Argives win your favour, offering sacrifices
in wide Troy? Why then are you so wroth with him, Zeus?
Answering her, cloud-gathering Zeus spoke:
My child, what word has escaped the barrier of your teeth!
How then could I forget godlike Odysseus,
who is beyond mortals in mind, and beyond all others offerings to the gods
immortal has given — the gods who hold the wide heaven?
But Poseidon the earth-holder is unrelentingly, forever
wrathful over the Cyclops, whom he blinded of his eye —
godlike Polyphemus, whose might is greatest
among all the Cyclopes; the nymph Thoosa bore him,
daughter of Phorcys, lord of the unresting sea,
having lain with Poseidon in the hollow caves.
Ever since then, Poseidon the earth-shaker, though Odysseus
he does not kill, yet drives him wandering from his native land.
But come, let all of us here take careful thought for
his homecoming, how he may return; and Poseidon will let go
his anger; for surely he will not be able, in the face of all
the immortal gods, to strive alone against their will.
Then the goddess, gleaming-eyed Athena, answered him:
O our father, son of Cronos, highest of rulers,
if indeed this is now pleasing to the blessed gods,
that wise Odysseus should return to his own home,
then let us send Hermes the guide, the slayer of Argus,
to the island of Ogygia, so that with all speed
he may tell the fair-tressed nymph our unerring counsel,
the homecoming of steadfast-hearted Odysseus, that he may return;
but I shall go to Ithaca, so that his son
I may rouse the more, and put strength in his heart
to call the long-haired Achaeans to assembly
and speak out against all the suitors, who forever
slaughter his thronging sheep and his shambling crook-horned cattle.
And I will send him to Sparta and to sandy Pylos
to seek news of the homecoming of his dear father, if perhaps he may hear of it,
and so that noble fame among men may be his.
So speaking, she bound beneath her feet her fair sandals,
immortal, golden, which carried her both over the water
and over the boundless land alike with the breath of the wind;
and she took her stout spear, tipped with sharp bronze,
heavy, great, and sturdy, with which she subdues the ranks of men —
of heroes, those with whom she of the mighty father is wroth.
And she went darting down from the peaks of Olympus,
and she stood in the land of Ithaca at the outer doors of Odysseus,
on the threshold of the courtyard; and in her palm she held the bronze spear,
in the likeness of a stranger, Mentes, leader of the Taphians.
And there she found the haughty suitors. They just then
were delighting their hearts with draughts before the doors,
sitting on the hides of oxen which they themselves had slain;
and heralds and busy attendants waited on them:
some were mixing wine and water in mixing-bowls,
while others with porous sponges were wiping the tables
and setting them forth, and others were dividing up meats in abundance.
And godlike Telemachus was far the first to see her,
for he sat among the suitors, grieved in his dear heart,
seeing his noble father in his mind's eye, if coming from somewhere
he might make a scattering of the suitors through the halls,
and himself hold his honour and be lord over his own house.
Thinking these things, sitting among the suitors, he caught sight of Athena.
And he went straight to the porch, for he was indignant at heart
that a stranger should stand long at the doors; and standing near her
he clasped her right hand and took from her the bronze spear,
and lifting his voice he addressed her with winged words:
"Greetings, stranger; among us you shall be made welcome; and then afterwards,
when you have tasted supper, you shall tell what you have need of."
So saying he led the way, and Pallas Athena followed.
And when they were inside the lofty house,
he carried the spear and set it against a tall pillar
inside the well-polished spear-rack, where indeed other
spears of steadfast-hearted Odysseus stood in numbers,
and her herself he led and seated on a chair, spreading a linen cloth beneath,
a beautiful chair, cunningly wrought; and beneath was a footstool for her feet.
And beside her he placed for himself an inlaid seat, apart from the others,
the suitors, lest the stranger, vexed by the din,
should take offence at the meal, having come among overweening men,
and so that he might ask him about his father who was gone.
And a handmaid brought hand-washing water and poured it from a pitcher,
a beautiful golden one, over a silver basin,
for washing; and beside them she drew up a polished table.
And the revered housekeeper brought bread and set it before them,
laying out many dishes besides, giving freely of what was at hand;
and a carver lifted up and set beside them platters of meats
of every sort, and beside them he placed golden cups;
and a herald came to them often, pouring the wine.
And in came the haughty suitors. They then
sat down in rows on the seats and chairs,
and heralds poured water over their hands,
and maidservants heaped up bread beside them in baskets,
and young men filled the mixing-bowls brim-full with drink.
And they put forth their hands to the good things lying ready before them.
But when they had put from them the desire for drink and food,
the suitors; their hearts were set on other things,
song and dancing, for these are the crowning graces of a feast;
and a herald placed a beautiful lyre in the hands
of Phemius, who sang for the suitors under compulsion.
And indeed, striking the strings, he struck up his beautiful song.
But Telemachus spoke to gleaming-eyed Athena,
holding his head close, so that the others might not hear:
"Dear stranger, will you be indignant at me for what I may say?
These things are what these men care for, the lyre and song,
easily, since they devour another man's livelihood without payment,
a man whose white bones now, I suppose, rot in the rain
lying on the mainland, or the wave rolls them in the sea.
If they should see that man returned home to Ithaca,
they would all pray to be lighter on their feet
rather than richer in gold and in raiment.
But as it is, he has perished thus by an evil doom, nor is there any
comfort for us, even if someone among men upon the earth
says he will come; his day of homecoming is lost.
But come, tell me this and recount it truly:
Who are you, and from where among men? Where are your city and your parents?
And on what sort of ship did you arrive? How did sailors
bring you to Ithaca? Who did they claim to be?
For I do not think you came here on foot at all.
And tell me this truly, so that I may know well,
whether you come here for the first time, or whether you are indeed a guest-friend
of my father's, since many men used to come to our house,
others too, since that man also went about among men."
Then the goddess, gleaming-eyed Athena, spoke to him in turn:
"Well then, I will tell you these things quite truly.
I declare that I am Mentes, son of wise-hearted Anchialus,
his son, and I rule over the oar-loving Taphians.
And now I have come down here thus with ship and companions,
sailing over the wine-dark sea to men of foreign speech,
to Temese for bronze, and I carry gleaming iron.
And my ship stands here by the countryside away from the city,
in the harbour of Rheithron, beneath wooded Neion.
And we declare that we are guest-friends of one another from our fathers
from of old, if indeed you go and ask the old man,
the hero Laertes, who they say no longer comes to the city
but far away in the countryside suffers woes
with an aged woman as his servant, who sets food and drink
before him, whenever weariness takes hold of his limbs
as he creeps along the slope of his vineyard plot.
And now I have come; for indeed they said he was at home among his people,
your father; but now the gods are thwarting him from his path.
For godlike Odysseus has not yet died upon the earth,
but still alive somewhere he is held back on the broad sea
on a sea-girt island, and harsh men hold him,
savages, who somewhere detain that man against his will.
But now I will prophesy to you, as in my heart
the immortals put it, and as I think it will be accomplished,
though I am no seer at all, nor one who knows birds of omen clearly.
Not much longer, surely, from his dear native land
will he be absent, not even if iron bonds hold him;
he will contrive how he may return, since he is a man of many devices.
But come, tell me this and recount it exactly,
whether, tall as you are, you are the very son of Odysseus himself.
Wonderfully indeed in your head and beautiful eyes you resemble
him, since so often we used to mingle with one another,
before he embarked for Troy, where also the others,
the best of the Argives, went in the hollow ships;
since then I have not seen Odysseus, nor he me.
Then wise Telemachus answered her in turn:
Well then, stranger, I will tell you very exactly.
My mother says that I am his; but I myself
do not know; for no one yet has of himself known his own begetting.
Would that I had been the son of some blessed
man, whom old age overtook among his own possessions.
But now, of him who was the most ill-fated of mortal men,
of him they say I was born, since you ask me this.
Then the goddess, gleaming-eyed Athena, spoke to him in turn:
Surely not nameless in time to come have the gods your lineage
made, since Penelope bore you to be such as you are.
But come, tell me this and recount it exactly:
What feast, what gathering is this? What need have you of it?
A banquet or a wedding? Since this is surely no meal where each brings his share;
so insolently and outrageously they seem to me
to feast throughout the house. A man would be indignant
at seeing so many shameful things, any man of sense who should come among them.
Then wise Telemachus answered her in turn:
Stranger, since indeed you ask me these things and inquire,
this house once bade fair to be rich and blameless
indeed, while that man was still at home among his people;
but now the gods have willed otherwise, devising evils,
they who have made that man unseen beyond all
men, since not even for his death would I grieve so,
if he had been slain among his comrades in the land of the Trojans,
or in the arms of his friends, when he had wound up the war.
Then the Panachaeans would have made him a tomb,
and for his son too he would have won great glory in time to come.
But now the storm-winds have snatched him away without glory;
he is gone, unseen, unheard of, and to me pains and lamentations
he has left. Nor do I mourn and groan for him
alone, since now the gods have wrought other evil cares for me.
For all the best men who hold sway over the islands,
over Dulichium and Same and wooded Zacynthus,
and all who lord it throughout rocky Ithaca,
so many woo my mother, and wear away the house.
And she neither refuses the hateful marriage nor an end
can she bring about; and they with their eating waste away
my own house; and soon they will tear me to pieces myself as well.
Then, stirred to anger, Pallas Athena spoke to him:
Alas, truly you have great need of Odysseus, who is gone away,
that he might lay his hands upon the shameless suitors.
For if only he would come now and at the outer doors of the house
take his stand, wearing helmet and shield and two spears,
being such a man as when I first knew him
in our house, drinking and making merry,
coming up from Ephyre, from the house of Ilus son of Mermerus—
for Odysseus went there too on his swift ship
seeking a man-slaying poison, so that he might have it
to smear on his bronze-tipped arrows; but that man would not
give it to him, since he stood in awe of the gods who are forever,
but my father gave it to him; for he loved him exceedingly—
being such a man, might Odysseus come among the suitors;
then would they all be swift-doomed and bitter in their marriage.
But truly these things lie on the knees of the gods,
whether he will return and take vengeance, or whether not,
in his own halls; but you I bid to take thought
how you may thrust the suitors out of the hall.
Come now, give heed and mark my words:
tomorrow call the Achaean heroes to assembly
and declare your word to all, and let the gods be witnesses.
Bid the suitors scatter to their own homes,
and as for your mother, if her heart urges her to marry,
let her go back to the hall of her father, a man of great power;
and they will prepare the wedding and make ready the bride-gifts,
very many, as many as ought to go with a beloved daughter.
And to you yourself I will give shrewd counsel, if you will heed it:
fit out a ship with twenty rowers, the best there is,
and go to seek news of your father, so long gone away,
in case some mortal may tell you of him, or you may hear a rumour
from Zeus, which most of all carries tidings to men.
First go to Pylos and question godlike Nestor,
and from there to Sparta, to fair-haired Menelaus;
for he came home last of the bronze-clad Achaeans.
If you hear that your father is alive and returning,
then, worn down though you are, you could endure for yet a year;
but if you hear that he is dead and no longer living,
then return to your dear fatherland
and heap up a mound for him and pay him funeral rites over it,
very many, as many as are fitting, and give your mother to a husband.
But when you have finished and accomplished these things,
then take thought in your mind and in your heart
how the suitors in your halls
you may kill, whether by guile or openly; nor is there any need for you
to cling to childish ways, since you are no longer of such an age.
Or have you not heard what fame godlike Orestes won
among all men, when he slew his father's murderer,
guileful Aegisthus, who had killed his glorious father?
And you, my friend — for I see you are very handsome and tall —
be valiant, so that many a man even of those born hereafter may speak well of you.
But I will now go down to my swift ship
and my comrades, who I think are chafing greatly as they wait for me;
but let this be your own concern, and take heed of my words.
Then wise Telemachus answered her in turn:
Stranger, truly you say these things with kindly intent,
as a father to his son, and never will I forget them.
But come now, stay a while, eager though you are for your journey,
so that, having bathed and delighted your own dear heart,
you may go to your ship with a gift, rejoicing in spirit,
a precious one, very beautiful, which shall be an heirloom for you
from me, such things as dear guest-friends give to guest-friends.
Then the goddess, gleaming-eyed Athena, answered him:
Do not hold me back any longer now, eager as I am for my journey.
And whatever gift your dear heart bids you give me,
give it to me when I come back again, to carry homeward,
and choose a right beautiful one; it will bring you a worthy return.
So then, having spoken thus, gleaming-eyed Athena departed,
and like a bird she flew away up through the smoke-vent; and in his heart
she put strength and courage, and reminded him of his father
even more than before. And he, taking note in his mind,
marvelled in his heart; for he guessed that it was a god.
And straightway he went among the suitors, a godlike man.
For them the far-famed bard was singing, and they in silence
sat listening; and he sang of the return of the Achaeans,
the woeful return which Pallas Athena laid on them out of Troy.
And from the upper chamber his wondrous song was heeded in her mind by
the daughter of Icarius, circumspect Penelope;
and she came down the high staircase of her house,
not alone — together with her there followed two handmaids as well.
And when she, shining among women, came to the suitors,
she stood beside the doorpost of the stoutly built roof,
holding her shining veil before her cheeks;
and a trusty handmaid stood on either side of her.
Then, bursting into tears, she addressed the divine bard:
Phemius, many other charms of mortals you know,
deeds of men and of gods, which bards make famous;
sit beside them and sing them one of those, and let them in silence
drink their wine; but cease from this song,
this woeful one, which always within my breast my dear heart
wears down, since grief beyond forgetting has come upon me above all.
For such a head do I long for, remembering him always,
a man whose fame is wide through Hellas and mid-Argos.
Then wise Telemachus answered her in turn:
My mother, why then do you begrudge the trusty bard
his delighting us in whatever way his mind is stirred? It is not the bards
who are to blame, but Zeus, I suppose, is to blame, who gives
to bread-eating men, just as he wills, to each one.
With this man there is no cause for anger, that he sings the evil doom of the Danaans;
for men give greater renown to that song
which comes newest to those who hear it.
So let your heart and spirit endure to listen;
for Odysseus was not the only one who lost his day of homecoming
in Troy, but many other men perished too.
But go into the house and attend to your own tasks,
the loom and the distaff, and bid your handmaids
go about their work; speech shall be men's concern,
all men's, and mine above all; for the power in this house is mine.
She, in amazement, went back into the house;
for she laid to heart the wise word of her son.
And going up to the upper chamber with her handmaid women
she then wept for Odysseus, her dear husband, until upon her
gleaming-eyed Athena cast sweet sleep on her eyelids.
But the suitors broke into uproar through the shadowy halls,
and all of them prayed to lie beside her in bed.
And among them wise Telemachus began to speak:
Suitors of my mother, possessed of overbearing insolence,
for now let us feast and take our pleasure, and let there be no
shouting, since this is a fine thing, to listen to a singer
such as this man is, like to the gods in voice.
But at dawn let us go and take our seats in the assembly,
all of us, so that I may declare my word to you outright:
get out of my halls; and busy yourselves with other feasts,
eating your own possessions, moving from house to house.
But if this seems to you the better and finer thing,
that one man's livelihood be destroyed without recompense,
then waste it; but I will cry out to the gods who are forever,
in the hope that Zeus may somehow grant that deeds of requital come to pass;
then you would perish within these halls unavenged.
So he spoke, and they all bit their lips
and marvelled at Telemachus, that he spoke so boldly.
Then Antinous answered him, the son of Eupeithes:
Telemachus, surely the gods themselves are teaching you
to be a lofty talker and to speak boldly;
may the son of Cronos never make you king in sea-girt Ithaca,
which is your inheritance by birth from your father.
Then wise Telemachus answered him in turn:
Antinous, will you indeed be indignant with me for whatever I say?
Even this I would be willing to accept, if Zeus should grant it.
Do you say this is the worst thing that can befall among men?
For it is no bad thing to be king; straightway one's house
grows rich and oneself more honoured.
But truly there are other kings of the Achaeans too,
many of them, in sea-girt Ithaca, young and old,
one of whom may hold this office, since noble Odysseus is dead;
but I will be lord of our own house
and of the slaves whom noble Odysseus won for me.
Then Eurymachus, son of Polybus, answered him in turn:
Telemachus, truly these things lie on the knees of the gods,
whoever shall be king over the Achaeans in seagirt Ithaca;
but may you yourself keep your possessions and be lord in your own house.
For never may that man come who against your will, by force,
shall wrest your possessions from you, while Ithaca is still a peopled land.
But I wish, noblest one, to ask you about the stranger,
from where that man comes, and from what land he claims to be
sprung, and where now are his kindred and his native ploughland.
Does he bring some tidings of your father's coming,
or does he arrive here seeking some business of his own?
How he sprang up and was gone in an instant, nor did he wait
to be known; and yet in his face he seemed no base man.
Then wise Telemachus answered him in turn:
Eurymachus, surely my father's homecoming is lost and gone;
so I no longer put trust in tidings, from wherever they may come,
nor do I heed any prophecy, whatever seer my mother
may summon into the hall and question.
But this stranger is a guest-friend of my father's house, from Taphos,
and he claims to be of wise Anchialus the
son, Mentes, and he is lord over the oar-loving Taphians.
So spoke Telemachus, but in his heart he knew the immortal goddess.
And they, turning to the dance and to lovely song,
took their pleasure, and waited for evening to come on.
And as they made merry, dark evening came upon them;
then at last they went, each man to his own house, to take their rest.
But Telemachus, where his chamber off the beautiful courtyard
had been built high, in a place with a clear view all round,
there he went to his bed, pondering many things in his heart.
And along with him, bearing blazing torches, went she of loyal thoughts,
Eurycleia, daughter of Ops, Peisenor's son,
whom once Laertes had bought with his own possessions,
while she was still in her first youth, and he gave the worth of twenty oxen,
and he honoured her equally with his faithful wife in his halls,
but never lay with her in love, and so he shunned his wife's anger;
she it was who bore the blazing torches beside him, and him most
of all the handmaids she loved, and she had nursed him when he was little.
And she opened the doors of the close-built chamber,
and he sat down on the bed and stripped off his soft tunic;
and this he cast into the hands of the shrewd old woman.
She, folding the tunic and smoothing it out,
hung it on a peg beside the corded bedstead,
then went her way from the chamber, and pulled the door to by its handle
of silver, and shot the bolt home with the strap.
There all night long, wrapped in a fleece of wool,
he pondered in his mind the journey that Athena had shown him.
