So speaking, she bound beneath her feet her fair sandals,
immortal, golden, which carried her both over the water
and over the boundless land alike with the breath of the wind;
and she took her stout spear, tipped with sharp bronze,
heavy, great, and sturdy, with which she subdues the ranks of men —
of heroes, those with whom she of the mighty father is wroth.
And she went darting down from the peaks of Olympus,
and she stood in the land of Ithaca at the outer doors of Odysseus,
on the threshold of the courtyard; and in her palm she held the bronze spear,
in the likeness of a stranger, Mentes, leader of the Taphians.
And there she found the haughty suitors. They just then
were delighting their hearts with draughts before the doors,
sitting on the hides of oxen which they themselves had slain;
and heralds and busy attendants waited on them:
some were mixing wine and water in mixing-bowls,
while others with porous sponges were wiping the tables
and setting them forth, and others were dividing up meats in abundance.
And godlike Telemachus was far the first to see her,
for he sat among the suitors, grieved in his dear heart,
seeing his noble father in his mind's eye, if coming from somewhere
he might make a scattering of the suitors through the halls,
and himself hold his honour and be lord over his own house.
Thinking these things, sitting among the suitors, he caught sight of Athena.
And he went straight to the porch, for he was indignant at heart
that a stranger should stand long at the doors; and standing near her
he clasped her right hand and took from her the bronze spear,
and lifting his voice he addressed her with winged words:
"Greetings, stranger; among us you shall be made welcome; and then afterwards,
when you have tasted supper, you shall tell what you have need of."
So saying he led the way, and Pallas Athena followed.
And when they were inside the lofty house,
he carried the spear and set it against a tall pillar
inside the well-polished spear-rack, where indeed other
spears of steadfast-hearted Odysseus stood in numbers,
and her herself he led and seated on a chair, spreading a linen cloth beneath,
a beautiful chair, cunningly wrought; and beneath was a footstool for her feet.
And beside her he placed for himself an inlaid seat, apart from the others,
the suitors, lest the stranger, vexed by the din,
should take offence at the meal, having come among overweening men,
and so that he might ask him about his father who was gone.
And a handmaid brought hand-washing water and poured it from a pitcher,
a beautiful golden one, over a silver basin,
for washing; and beside them she drew up a polished table.
And the revered housekeeper brought bread and set it before them,
laying out many dishes besides, giving freely of what was at hand;
and a carver lifted up and set beside them platters of meats
of every sort, and beside them he placed golden cups;
and a herald came to them often, pouring the wine.
And in came the haughty suitors. They then
sat down in rows on the seats and chairs,
and heralds poured water over their hands,
and maidservants heaped up bread beside them in baskets,
and young men filled the mixing-bowls brim-full with drink.
And they put forth their hands to the good things lying ready before them.
But when they had put from them the desire for drink and food,
the suitors; their hearts were set on other things,
song and dancing, for these are the crowning graces of a feast;
and a herald placed a beautiful lyre in the hands
of Phemius, who sang for the suitors under compulsion.
And indeed, striking the strings, he struck up his beautiful song.
But Telemachus spoke to gleaming-eyed Athena,
holding his head close, so that the others might not hear:
"Dear stranger, will you be indignant at me for what I may say?
These things are what these men care for, the lyre and song,
easily, since they devour another man's livelihood without payment,
a man whose white bones now, I suppose, rot in the rain
lying on the mainland, or the wave rolls them in the sea.
If they should see that man returned home to Ithaca,
they would all pray to be lighter on their feet
rather than richer in gold and in raiment.
But as it is, he has perished thus by an evil doom, nor is there any
comfort for us, even if someone among men upon the earth
says he will come; his day of homecoming is lost.
But come, tell me this and recount it truly:
Who are you, and from where among men? Where are your city and your parents?
And on what sort of ship did you arrive? How did sailors
bring you to Ithaca? Who did they claim to be?
For I do not think you came here on foot at all.
And tell me this truly, so that I may know well,
whether you come here for the first time, or whether you are indeed a guest-friend
of my father's, since many men used to come to our house,
others too, since that man also went about among men."
Then the goddess, gleaming-eyed Athena, spoke to him in turn:
"Well then, I will tell you these things quite truly.
I declare that I am Mentes, son of wise-hearted Anchialus,
his son, and I rule over the oar-loving Taphians.
And now I have come down here thus with ship and companions,
sailing over the wine-dark sea to men of foreign speech,
to Temese for bronze, and I carry gleaming iron.
And my ship stands here by the countryside away from the city,
in the harbour of Rheithron, beneath wooded Neion.
And we declare that we are guest-friends of one another from our fathers
from of old, if indeed you go and ask the old man,
the hero Laertes, who they say no longer comes to the city
but far away in the countryside suffers woes
with an aged woman as his servant, who sets food and drink
before him, whenever weariness takes hold of his limbs
as he creeps along the slope of his vineyard plot.
And now I have come; for indeed they said he was at home among his people,
your father; but now the gods are thwarting him from his path.
For godlike Odysseus has not yet died upon the earth,
but still alive somewhere he is held back on the broad sea
on a sea-girt island, and harsh men hold him,
savages, who somewhere detain that man against his will.
But now I will prophesy to you, as in my heart
the immortals put it, and as I think it will be accomplished,
though I am no seer at all, nor one who knows birds of omen clearly.
Not much longer, surely, from his dear native land
will he be absent, not even if iron bonds hold him;
he will contrive how he may return, since he is a man of many devices.
But come, tell me this and recount it exactly,
whether, tall as you are, you are the very son of Odysseus himself.
Wonderfully indeed in your head and beautiful eyes you resemble
him, since so often we used to mingle with one another,
before he embarked for Troy, where also the others,
the best of the Argives, went in the hollow ships;
since then I have not seen Odysseus, nor he me.
Then wise Telemachus answered her in turn:
Well then, stranger, I will tell you very exactly.
My mother says that I am his; but I myself
do not know; for no one yet has of himself known his own begetting.
Would that I had been the son of some blessed
man, whom old age overtook among his own possessions.
But now, of him who was the most ill-fated of mortal men,
of him they say I was born, since you ask me this.
Then the goddess, gleaming-eyed Athena, spoke to him in turn:
Surely not nameless in time to come have the gods your lineage
made, since Penelope bore you to be such as you are.
But come, tell me this and recount it exactly:
What feast, what gathering is this? What need have you of it?
A banquet or a wedding? Since this is surely no meal where each brings his share;
so insolently and outrageously they seem to me
to feast throughout the house. A man would be indignant
at seeing so many shameful things, any man of sense who should come among them.
