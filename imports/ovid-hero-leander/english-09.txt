So, when daylight has passed and the friendlier hour of night
has revealed the bright stars after driving away the day,
at once we place watchful lights on the highest roof,
the signs and marker of your familiar route,
and, drawing twisted threads with the turning spindle,
we cheat the slow delay with a woman’s craft.
You ask what I say meanwhile through so long a time?
Nothing but Leander’s name is in my mouth.
“Do you think my delight has now left home, nurse,
or is everyone awake and is he afraid of his family?
Do you think he is now taking his clothes from his shoulders
and now anointing his limbs with rich oil?”
She usually nods—not because she cares about our kisses,
but because creeping sleep moves her aged head.
After the shortest delay I say, “Now he is surely swimming
and throwing his supple arms through the parted water.”
When I have completed a few threads and the spindle has touched the ground,
we ask whether you might be in mid-strait.
Now we look ahead, now we pray in a timid voice
that a helpful breeze may give you an easy path;
with our ears we catch uncertain sounds and believe every
noise to be the sound of your arrival.
