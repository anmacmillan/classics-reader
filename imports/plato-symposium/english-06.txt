Then Socrates said: You have fought your own contest finely, Eryximachus;
but if you were in the position I am now in — or rather, perhaps, the one I shall be in when Agathon too has spoken well — you would be very frightened indeed, and utterly at a loss, just as I am now.
You want to cast a spell on me, Socrates, said Agathon, so that I may be thrown into confusion by the thought that the theatre holds great expectation of my speaking well.
I should be forgetful indeed, Agathon, said Socrates, if, having seen your courage and high-mindedness as you mounted the platform with your actors and looked out to face so vast an audience, about to display compositions of your own, and were not in the least dismayed, I now supposed you would be flustered on account of us few men.
What, Socrates?
said Agathon; surely you do not think me so stuffed full of the theatre as not to know that, to a man of sense, a few wise people are more formidable than many fools?
I should certainly not be doing well, Agathon, he said, if I held any boorish opinion about you;
no, I know well that if you met with any men you considered wise, you would care more for them than for the many.
But we, I fear, are not those wise men — for we too were present there, and were part of the many — yet if you met with others who were wise, you would probably feel shame before them, if you thought you were perhaps doing something shameful;
or how do you mean it?
What you say is true, he said.
But before the many you would feel no shame, if you thought you were doing something shameful?
And at that, he said, Phaedrus broke in and said: My dear Agathon, if you keep answering Socrates, it will no longer make the slightest difference to him how anything here turns out in any way whatever, so long as he has someone to converse with — above all someone beautiful.
Now I listen with pleasure to Socrates conversing, but I am obliged to look after the encomium to Eros and to collect a speech from each one of you;
so let each of the two of you first pay what he owes to the god, and then converse to your hearts' content.
Well, you are right, Phaedrus, said Agathon, and nothing prevents me from speaking;
for there will be many another chance to converse with Socrates.
I wish first to say how I ought to speak, and then to speak.
For all who have spoken before me seem to me not to be praising the god, but to be felicitating mankind on the good things of which the god is for them the cause;
but what manner of being he himself is who bestowed these gifts, no one has said.
Yet there is one correct method for every praise of every subject: to set forth in speech what manner of being he is, and of what things he is the cause, whom the speech concerns.
In this way, then, it is right for us too to praise Eros — first what he himself is, and then his gifts.
I say, therefore, that though all the gods are happy, Eros — if it be lawful and free from offence to say so — is the happiest among them, being the most beautiful and the best.
And most beautiful he is, being of this character.
First, Phaedrus, he is the youngest of the gods.
And a great proof of this claim he himself supplies, fleeing in headlong flight from old age — a swift thing, clearly;
at any rate it comes upon us faster than it should.
This it is Eros's nature to hate, and not to approach it even from a great way off.
But with the young he ever keeps company, and young he ever is;
for the old saying holds good, that like draws ever near to like.
And I, though agreeing with Phaedrus on many other points, do not agree with him in this — that Eros is more ancient than Kronos and Iapetus; rather, I say that he is the youngest of the gods and ever young, and that those ancient doings among the gods, of which Hesiod and Parmenides tell, came about through Necessity and not through Eros, if those men spoke the truth;
for there would have been no castrations or bindings of one another, nor those many other deeds of violence, had Eros been among them, but friendship and peace, as now, ever since Eros has reigned as king over the gods.
Young, then, he is, and besides being young, delicate;
but he stands in need of a poet such as Homer was, to display the delicacy of a god.
For Homer says that Ate is both a god and delicate — her feet, at least, are delicate — when he says: delicate indeed are her feet;
for not upon the ground does she draw near, but lo, upon the heads of men she walks.
By a fine proof, then, he seems to me to demonstrate her delicacy: that she walks not upon what is hard, but upon what is soft.
The same proof, then, we too shall use concerning Eros, that he is delicate.
For he walks not upon the earth, nor upon skulls — which are not exactly soft — but in the softest of all things that are he both walks and dwells.
For in the characters and souls of gods and men he has founded his dwelling — and not, again, in all souls without distinction, but whenever he encounters a soul possessed of a hard character, he departs; where it is soft, there he makes his home.
Touching, therefore, ever with his feet and in every way the softest parts of the softest things, he must of necessity be most delicate.
Youngest, then, he is, and most delicate, and in addition to these supple of form.
For he could not fold himself about us on every side, nor pass unperceived through every soul, both entering at first and departing, if he were hard.
Of his well-proportioned and supple form a great proof is his gracefulness, a quality which, by the agreement of all, Eros possesses beyond all others;
for between gracelessness and Eros there is everlasting war with one another.
And the beauty of his complexion is signified by the god's dwelling among flowers;
for upon what is flowerless and past its bloom, whether body or soul or anything else whatsoever, Eros does not settle; but wherever a place is flowery and fragrant, there he both settles and remains.
Concerning the beauty of the god, then, even this much is sufficient — and yet much still remains — but after this one must speak of the virtue of Eros: the greatest point being that Eros neither wrongs nor is wronged, neither by god nor against god, neither by man nor against man.
For neither does he himself suffer by force, if he suffers anything at all— for force does not touch Eros;
nor when he acts does he act by force— for everyone serves Eros willingly in everything, and whatever one willing party agrees with another willing party, 'the laws, those kings of the city,' declare to be just.
And besides justice, he has the fullest share of moderation.
For it is agreed that moderation is the mastery of pleasures and desires, and that no pleasure is stronger than Eros;
and if they are weaker, they would be mastered by Eros, and he would be their master— and mastering pleasures and desires, Eros would be moderate beyond all others.
And what is more, in courage 'not even Ares stands against' Eros.
For it is not Ares who holds Eros, but Eros who holds Ares— love of Aphrodite, as the story goes— and the holder is stronger than the held;
and mastering the bravest of all the rest, he would be bravest of them all.
Now then, of the god's justice and moderation and courage I have spoken; his wisdom remains;
so, as far as possible, I must try to leave nothing lacking.
And first— so that I in my turn may honour our own art, as Eryximachus honoured his— the god is a poet so skilled that he can make another one too;
at any rate, everyone becomes a poet, 'even if he was a stranger to the Muses before,' once Eros touches him.
And this it is fitting for us to use as evidence that Eros is a good poet— a maker, in sum, in every kind of making that belongs to the Muses' art;
for what a man does not have, or does not know, he could neither give to another nor teach anyone else.
And indeed, as for the making of all living creatures, who will deny that this is the wisdom of Eros, by which all living things come to be and grow?
And as for craftsmanship in the arts, do we not know that whoever has this god for a teacher turns out famous and brilliant, while whomever Eros does not touch stays in the dark?
Archery, at any rate, and medicine and prophecy Apollo discovered with desire and love as his guides, so that he too would be a pupil of Eros, as are the Muses in music, and Hephaestus in bronze-working, and Athena in weaving, and Zeus in 'the steering of gods and men.'
Hence indeed the affairs of the gods were set in order once Eros had come to be among them— love of beauty, plainly, for there is no love where ugliness is— whereas before that, as I said at the beginning, many dreadful things befell the gods, so the story goes, through the kingship of Necessity;
but from the moment this god was born, from the loving of beautiful things all good things have come to be, for gods and for men alike.
Thus it seems to me, Phaedrus, that Eros, being himself first the most beautiful and the best, is thereafter the cause for others of other things of the same kind.
And something moves me to speak in verse as well— that it is he who makes 'peace among men, on the open sea a windless calm, a couch for the winds, and sleep upon sorrow.'
It is he who empties us of estrangement and fills us with kinship, appointing all gatherings like this one where we come together with one another, making himself our leader in feasts, in dances, in sacrifices;
supplying gentleness, expelling wildness;
lavish of goodwill, giftless of ill-will;
gracious and good;
gazed upon by the wise, marvelled at by the gods;
coveted by those who have no share in him, treasured by those blessed with a share;
father of luxury, of delicacy, of splendour, of graces, of longing, of desire;
careful of the good, careless of the bad;
in toil, in fear, in yearning, in discourse the best steersman, shipmate, comrade-in-arms, and saviour; the ornament of all gods and men together, the leader most beautiful and best, whom every man must follow, hymning him beautifully, sharing in the song he sings as he charms the mind of all gods and men.
Let this speech of mine, Phaedrus, he said, be dedicated to the god— partaking partly of play, partly of measured seriousness, so far as lies in my power.
