And when Agathon had spoken, Aristodemus said, all those present burst into applause, feeling that the young man had spoken in a manner worthy both of himself and of the god.
Then Socrates, glancing at Eryximachus, said: 'Do I seem to you now, son of Acumenus,' he said, 'to have long been fearing a fear with nothing to fear in it — or rather to have spoken like a prophet when I said just now that Agathon would speak marvellously and that I would be at a loss?'
'On the one point,' said Eryximachus, 'you seem to me to have spoken prophetically — that Agathon would speak well;
but that you will be at a loss — that I do not believe.'
'And how, my blessed friend,' said Socrates, 'am I not bound to be at a loss — I or anyone else whatsoever — having to speak after so beautiful and so richly varied a speech has been delivered?'
And the rest of it was not marvellous to quite the same degree;
but that ending — who would not have been struck dumb, hearing the beauty of its words and phrases?
For my part, reflecting that I myself would not be able to say anything beautiful that came even close to these, I nearly ran off and vanished for shame — if I had had anywhere to go.
For the speech kept reminding me of Gorgias, so that I quite simply suffered the fate in Homer:
I was afraid that Agathon, ending his speech, would send against my speech the head of Gorgias — that dread master of speaking — and turn me myself to very stone with speechlessness.
And I realised then how ridiculous I had been, all along, when I agreed to take my turn with you in praising Eros and claimed to be terribly clever in matters of love, knowing, as it turns out, nothing of the business — of how one ought to praise anything at all.
For I, in my simplicity, supposed that one ought to tell the truth about each thing being praised, and that this should be the foundation, and that, selecting from these very truths the most beautiful points, one should arrange them as becomingly as possible;
and I was feeling quite proud of myself, sure I would speak well, since I knew the truth about praising anything.
But this, it appears, was not after all what it is to praise anything finely; rather, it is to attribute to the subject the greatest and most beautiful qualities possible, whether it really is so or not;
and if they are false, well, no matter after all.
For what was proposed, it seems, was that each of us should seem to praise Eros, not that he should actually praise him.
That, I suppose, is why you stir up every possible story and attribute it all to Eros, and declare him to be of such a kind and the cause of so much, so that he may appear as beautiful and as good as possible — to those who do not know him, obviously, for surely not to those who do — and a fine, stately piece of praise it is.
But I, you see, simply did not know this manner of praising, and it was in that ignorance that I agreed with you that I too would offer praise when my turn came.
So it was my tongue that promised; my mind did not:
let it go, then.
For I am no longer giving an encomium in that manner — I could not do it — but the truth, if you like, I am willing to tell in my own fashion, not in competition with your speeches, so that I do not make myself a laughing-stock.
Consider then, Phaedrus, whether you have any use for a speech of that sort as well — to hear the truth told about Eros, in whatever wording and arrangement of phrases happens to come to me as I go.
So Phaedrus, he said, and the others urged him to speak in whatever way he himself thought he ought to speak.
'One thing more then, Phaedrus,' he said: 'allow me to ask Agathon a few small questions, so that once I have secured his agreement I may then speak on that basis.'
'Well, I allow it,' said Phaedrus; 'ask away.'
After that, then, he said, Socrates began from somewhere about here.
'Truly, my dear Agathon, I thought you led into your speech beautifully, when you said that one must first display what sort of being Eros himself is, and afterwards his works.
That beginning I admire very much.
Come then, since you went through everything else about what Eros is like so beautifully and magnificently, tell me this too:
is Eros such as to be love of something, or of nothing?
I am not asking whether he is the love of some mother or father — for the question whether Eros is love of a mother or a father would be absurd — but as if I were asking about this very word "father": is the father father of something, or not?
You would surely have told me, if you wished to answer properly, that the father is father of a son or of a daughter;
or would you not?'
'Certainly,' said Agathon.
'And the mother likewise?'
This too was agreed.
'Then answer a little more,' said Socrates, 'so that you may better grasp what I am after.
Suppose I were to ask: what about this —
a brother, in respect of the very thing he is, is he brother of someone, or not?'
He said that he is.
'Of a brother, that is, or of a sister?'
He agreed.
'Try then,' he said, 'to say the same of love:
is Eros love of nothing, or of something?'
'Of something, most certainly.'
'Well then,' said Socrates, 'keep this guarded in your mind, remembering what it is love of;
and tell me just this much: does Eros desire that of which he is love, or not?'
'Certainly,' he said.
Is it when he has the very thing he desires and loves that he then desires and loves it, or when he does not have it?
When he does not have it, in all likelihood, he said.
Consider then, said Socrates, whether instead of 'in all likelihood' it is not necessarily so: that what desires, desires what it lacks, and does not desire unless it is in lack.
To me, Agathon, it seems wonderfully clear that it is necessary;
how does it seem to you?
To me too, he said, it seems so.
Well said.
Would anyone, then, being tall, wish to be tall, or being strong, wish to be strong?
Impossible, from what has been agreed.
For surely the man who is these things would not be lacking in them.
True.
For if a man who is strong should wish to be strong, said Socrates, and one who is swift to be swift, and one who is healthy to be healthy — for perhaps someone might suppose, in these and all such cases, that those who are of this sort and possess these things also desire the very things they possess; and so I say this so that we are not misled — for these men, Agathon, if you think about it, must necessarily have, in the present, each of the things they have, whether they wish it or not; and who, surely, would desire that?
But when someone says, 'I, who am healthy, wish also to be healthy; I, who am rich, wish also to be rich; and I desire the very things I have,' we would say to him: 'You, my good man, possessing wealth and health and strength, wish to possess these things in the future as well, since in the present at least, whether you wish it or not, you have them;'
'consider, then, whenever you say this, that you desire what is present to you, whether you mean anything other than this: that you wish the things now present to be present in the future as well.'
He would agree, would he not?
Agathon, he said, assented.
Then Socrates said: So this is loving that which is not yet ready to hand for him, and which he does not have — that these things be preserved for him and present in the time to come?
Certainly, he said.
So this man, and everyone else who desires, desires what is not ready to hand and not present; and what he does not have, and what he himself is not, and what he lacks — such are the things of which desire and love are?
Certainly, he said.
Come then, said Socrates, let us recapitulate what has been said.
Love is, is he not, first of all love of certain things, and then of those things whose lack is present in him?
Yes, he said.
With that settled, recall what things you said in your speech Love is of;
or, if you like, I will remind you.
For I think you said something like this: that matters were set in order among the gods through love of beautiful things;
for there could be no love of ugly things.
Wasn't that roughly what you said?
I did say it, said Agathon.
And quite reasonably too, my friend, said Socrates;
and if that is so, Love would be love of beauty, and not of ugliness, would he not?
He agreed.
And has it not been agreed that he loves that which he lacks and does not have?
Yes, he said.
Then Love lacks beauty and does not have it.
Necessarily, he said.
What then?
Do you say that what lacks beauty and in no way possesses beauty is beautiful?
Certainly not.
Do you still agree, then, that Love is beautiful, if this is how things stand?
And Agathon said: It looks, Socrates, as if I knew nothing of what I said then.
And yet you spoke beautifully, Agathon, he said.
But tell me one small thing more:
do you not think that good things are also beautiful?
I do.
Then if Love lacks beautiful things, and good things are beautiful, he would lack good things too.
I, Socrates, he said, could not contradict you; so let it be as you say.
No: it is the truth, he said, my beloved Agathon, that you cannot contradict, since to contradict Socrates is not hard at all.
And you, for your part, I shall now let go;
but the account of Eros which I once heard from a woman of Mantinea, Diotima — who was wise in these matters and in many others, and who once, when the Athenians offered sacrifice before the plague, brought about a ten-year postponement of the disease, and it was she who taught me the things of love — the account, then, that she used to give, I shall try to go through for you on the basis of what has been agreed between me and Agathon, by myself, on my own, as best I can.
It is necessary, then, Agathon, just as you laid it out, first to go through who Eros is and of what sort he is, and then his works.
Now it seems to me easiest to go through it in the way the stranger-woman once went through it when she was examining me.
For I myself was saying to her pretty much the same sort of things as Agathon was saying to me just now — that Eros was a great god, and that he was of beautiful things;
and she proceeded to refute me with the very arguments with which I refuted him: that by my own account he was neither beautiful nor good.
And I said, 'How do you mean, Diotima?
Is Eros then ugly and bad?'
And she — 'Will you not hold your tongue from ill-omened words?'
she said;
'or do you suppose that whatever is not beautiful must necessarily be ugly?'
