From there he marched two stages, ten parasangs, to the river Psarus, which was three plethra wide.
From there he marched one stage, five parasangs, to the river Pyramus, which was a stade wide.
From there he marched two stages, fifteen parasangs, to Issus, the last city of Cilicia, situated on the sea, large and prosperous.
There they stayed for three days;
and the thirty-five ships from the Peloponnese reached Cyrus, with the Spartan Pythagoras in command of them as admiral.
They were guided from Ephesus by Tamos the Egyptian, who had another twenty-five ships belonging to Cyrus, with which he had besieged Miletus when it was friendly to Tissaphernes, and had fought on Cyrus’ side against him.
Chirisophus the Spartan was also there on the ships, summoned by Cyrus, with seven hundred hoplites, whom he commanded in Cyrus’ service.
The ships lay at anchor beside Cyrus’ tent.
There too the Greek mercenaries serving with Abrocomas deserted and came over to Cyrus, four hundred hoplites, and joined the campaign against the King.
From there he marched one stage, five parasangs, to the gates of Cilicia and Syria.
These were two walls: the inner one, in front of Cilicia, was held by Syennesis and a garrison of Cilicians, while the outer one, in front of Syria, was said to be guarded by a garrison of the King.
Between them flows a river called the Carsus, a plethron wide.
The whole distance between the walls was three stades;
and it was impossible to get through by force;
for the pass was narrow and the walls ran down to the sea, and above were sheer rocks;
and in both walls gates had been set.
It was because of this pass that Cyrus had sent for the ships, so that he could land hoplites inside and outside the gates and force his way past the enemy if they were guarding the Syrian gates, as Cyrus expected Abrocomas to do, since he had a large army.
But Abrocomas did not do this; when he heard that Cyrus was in Cilicia, he turned back from Phoenicia and marched off to join the King, with an army, so it was said, of three hundred thousand men.
From there he marched through Syria one stage, five parasangs, to Myriandus, a city on the sea inhabited by Phoenicians;
the place was a trading port, and many merchant ships lay at anchor there.
