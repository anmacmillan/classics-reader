Across the river Euphrates, opposite the desert stages, there was a prosperous and large city named Charmande;
from this city the soldiers bought their supplies, crossing on rafts in the following way.
They filled the hides they had as tent covers with light hay, then drew them together and stitched them up, so that the water would not touch the straw;
on these they crossed and got their supplies: wine made from the fruit of the date palm, and millet grain;
for this was what the country had most of.
A dispute arose there between some of Menon’s soldiers and some of Clearchus’, and Clearchus, judging that Menon’s man was in the wrong, gave him a beating;
the man went to his own troops and told them;
when the soldiers heard it, they were indignant and very angry with Clearchus.
On the same day Clearchus, after going to the crossing-place of the river and inspecting the market there, rode back to his own tent through Menon’s camp with a few of his attendants;
Cyrus had not yet arrived, but was still riding up;
one of Menon’s soldiers, who was splitting wood, when he saw Clearchus riding through, threw his axe at him;
this man missed him;
but another threw a stone, and another, then many, as a shout went up.
He fled to his own troops and at once gave the order to take up arms;
he ordered his hoplites to stay where they were, resting their shields against their knees, while he himself took the Thracians and the horsemen he had in his army, more than forty, most of them Thracians, and rode at Menon’s men, so that they and Menon himself were terrified and ran to their arms;
some even stood still, at a loss what to do.
But Proxenus (for he happened to be coming up behind, with a company of hoplites following him) at once led them into the space between the two sides, grounded arms, and begged Clearchus not to do this.
But Clearchus was angry that, when he himself had come close to being stoned to death, Proxenus spoke calmly of what he had suffered, and he told him to get out of the way.
At this moment Cyrus too came up and learned what had happened;
at once he took his javelins in his hands and rode with those of his trusted men who were present into the middle, and said this.
Clearchus and Proxenus and you other Greeks here, you do not know what you are doing.
For if you start a fight with one another, be sure that on this very day I shall be cut to pieces, and you not long after me;
for if our affairs go badly, all these barbarians you see will be more hostile to us than those who are with the king.
When Clearchus heard this, he came to his senses;
and both sides stopped and grounded their arms in their places.
