As they advanced from there, tracks and dung of horses appeared;
the trail was reckoned to be that of about two thousand horses.
These horsemen, as they went ahead, were burning both the fodder and anything else that was useful.
Orontas, a Persian, related to the king by birth and said to be among the best of the Persians in war, plotted against Cyrus, though he had fought against him before and then been reconciled.
He told Cyrus that if he gave him a thousand horsemen, he would either ambush and kill the horsemen who were burning ahead of them, or capture many of them alive and stop them burning as they advanced, and would make sure that they could never see Cyrus’ army and report it to the king.
When Cyrus heard this, it seemed useful to him, and he told him to take a detachment from each of the commanders.
Orontas, thinking that he had the horsemen ready for him, wrote a letter to the king saying that he would come with as many horsemen as he could;
and he asked him to tell his own horsemen to receive him as a friend.
In the letter there were also reminders of his former friendship and loyalty.
He gave this letter to a man he thought trustworthy;
but the man took it and gave it to Cyrus.
After reading it Cyrus arrested Orontas and summoned to his own tent the seven noblest Persians of his entourage, and ordered the Greek generals to bring hoplites and have them take up position under arms around his tent.
They did this, bringing about three thousand hoplites.
He also invited Clearchus inside as an adviser, since both Cyrus and the others thought he was held in the highest honour of the Greeks.
When he came out, he reported to his friends how the trial of Orontas had gone;
for it was not secret.
