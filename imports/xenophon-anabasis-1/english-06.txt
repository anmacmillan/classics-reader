From there they tried to force their way into Cilicia;
the entrance was a road just wide enough for a wagon, extremely steep and impossible for an army to get through if anyone opposed it.
It was also said that Syennesis was on the heights, guarding the pass;
so he waited a day in the plain.
The next day a messenger came saying that Syennesis had abandoned the heights, since he had learned that Menon's army was already in Cilicia, inside the mountains, and because he heard that triremes were sailing round from Ionia to Cilicia with Tamos in command, the Spartans' ships and Cyrus' own.
So Cyrus went up into the mountains without opposition and saw the tents where the Cilicians had been keeping guard.
From there he went down into a large and beautiful plain, well watered and full of trees of every kind and of vines;
it also produces plenty of sesame, millet, panic grass, wheat and barley.
A strong, high mountain range surrounds it on every side, from sea to sea.
Having come down, he marched through this plain four stages, twenty-five parasangs, to Tarsus, a large and prosperous city of Cilicia, where the palace of Syennesis, the king of the Cilicians, stood;
through the middle of the city flows a river called the Cydnus, two plethra wide.
The inhabitants had abandoned this city with Syennesis for a stronghold in the mountains, except for those who kept the taverns;
those who lived by the sea in Soli and Issi also stayed.
Epyaxa, the wife of Syennesis, arrived in Tarsus five days before Cyrus;
but in the crossing of the mountains down into the plain two companies of Menon's army were lost;
some said that they had been cut down by the Cilicians while plundering, others that they had been left behind and, unable to find the rest of the army or the roads, had then wandered about and perished;
at any rate, there were a hundred hoplites of them.
When the others arrived, angry at the loss of their comrades, they plundered the city of Tarsus and the palace in it.
When Cyrus had ridden into the city, he sent for Syennesis to come to him;
but Syennesis said that he had never yet put himself in the hands of anyone stronger than himself, and he was not willing to go to Cyrus then either, until his wife persuaded him and he received pledges of safety.
After this, when they had met each other, Syennesis gave Cyrus a great deal of money for the army, and Cyrus gave him the gifts that are held in honour at the King’s court, a horse with a golden bridle, a golden necklace, bracelets, a golden short sword and a Persian robe, and promised that his land would no longer be plundered;
and that the slaves who had been seized, wherever they came across them, they would get back.
