At this moment the barbarian army was advancing steadily, while the Greek army, still standing in the same place, was forming up from those who were still coming in.
And Cyrus, riding along at some distance from his army, was surveying the field, looking in both directions, at the enemy and at his friends.
Xenophon the Athenian saw him from the Greek lines, rode up to meet him and asked whether he had any orders;
Cyrus pulled up and told him to tell everyone that the omens were good and the sacrifices were good.
As he was saying this he heard a murmur going through the ranks, and asked what the noise was.
Xenophon said that the watchword was being passed along, for the second time now.
He wondered who had given it, and asked what the watchword was.
He answered:
‘Zeus Saviour and Victory.’
When Cyrus heard this, he said: ‘Well, I accept it, and so let it be.’
After saying this he rode back to his own position.
The two battle lines were no longer three or four stades apart when the Greeks sang the paean and began to advance against the enemy.
As they moved forward, part of the phalanx surged ahead, and the part left behind began to run;
and at the same moment they all raised the cry that men raise to Enyalius, and all began to run.
Some say that they also clashed their shields against their spears, to frighten the horses.
Before an arrow could reach them, the barbarians gave way and fled.
Then the Greeks pursued with all their might, but shouted to one another not to run, but to follow in formation.
The chariots were carried along, some through the enemy's own ranks, others through the Greeks, empty of their drivers.
Whenever the Greeks saw them coming, they opened their ranks;
one man was even caught, like someone dazed at a racecourse;
yet they said that even he came to no harm, nor did any other Greek suffer any harm at all in this battle, except that one man on the left wing was said to have been hit by an arrow.
