It was already about the time when the market is full, and the stopping place where he meant to halt was near, when Pategyas, a Persian, one of Cyrus' trusted men, came into view riding at full speed on a sweating horse, and at once shouted to everyone he met, in both Persian and Greek, that the king was approaching with a large army, ready as if for battle.
Then there was great confusion;
for the Greeks, and everyone else too, thought that he would fall on them at once while they were out of formation;
Cyrus leapt down from his chariot, put on his breastplate, mounted his horse and took his javelins in his hands, and he ordered all the others to arm themselves and each to take up his own position.
Then they took their positions in great haste, Clearchus holding the right end of the wing next to the river Euphrates, Proxenus next to him, the others after him, and Menon and his army held the left wing of the Greek force.
Of the barbarian force, Paphlagonian horsemen, about a thousand, stood beside Clearchus on the right, together with the Greek peltasts; on the left were Ariaeus, Cyrus' second-in-command, and the rest of the barbarian force; Cyrus and his horsemen, about six hundred, were in the centre, the men themselves armed with breastplates, thigh-guards and helmets, all except Cyrus;
Cyrus took his place for the battle with his head bare; it is said that the other Persians too face the dangers of war with bare heads.
All the horses with Cyrus had both frontlets and breastplates;
The horsemen also carried Greek sabres.
It was already midday, and the enemy were not yet in sight;
but when afternoon came, dust appeared like a white cloud, and a good while later something like a blackness spreading far across the plain.
As they came nearer, soon bronze began to flash here and there, and spear points, and the ranks became visible.
There were horsemen with white breastplates on the enemy's left;
Tissaphernes was said to command them;
next to them were men with wicker shields, and next to these hoplites with wooden shields reaching to their feet.
These were said to be Egyptians;
then more horsemen, then more archers.
All these marched by nations, each nation in a solid square packed with men.
In front of them were chariots, at wide intervals from one another, the so-called scythed chariots;
they had scythes stretched out sideways from the axles and also under the driver's seats pointing to the ground, so as to cut through whatever they met.
The plan was that they would drive into the ranks of the Greeks and cut them to pieces.
What Cyrus had said, however, when he called the Greeks together and urged them to stand firm against the shouting of the barbarians, proved false;
for they came on not with shouting but in the greatest possible silence, quietly, evenly and slowly.
At this point Cyrus himself rode along the line with Pigres the interpreter and three or four others, and shouted to Clearchus to lead the army against the enemy's centre, because the king was there;
‘And if we win there,’ he said, ‘we have done everything.’
Clearchus saw the massed centre and heard from Cyrus that the king was beyond the Greek left (for the king was so superior in numbers that, although he held the centre of his own army, he was beyond Cyrus' left); but even so Clearchus was not willing to draw the right wing away from the river, for fear of being surrounded on both sides, and he replied to Cyrus that he was taking care that all would be well.
