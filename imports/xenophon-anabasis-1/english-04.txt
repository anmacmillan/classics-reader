From there he marched three stages, twenty parasangs, to Celaenae, an inhabited city of Phrygia, large and prosperous.
Cyrus had a palace there and a large park full of wild animals, which he used to hunt on horseback whenever he wanted to exercise himself and his horses.
Through the middle of the park flows the river Maeander;
its springs rise in the palace;
and it also flows through the city of Celaenae.
There is also a fortified palace of the Great King in Celaenae, at the springs of the river Marsyas, below the citadel;
this river too flows through the city and empties into the Maeander;
the width of the Marsyas is twenty-five feet.
Here Apollo is said to have flayed Marsyas, after defeating him when he challenged him to a contest of skill, and to have hung up his skin in the cave where the springs rise;
and that is why the river is called Marsyas.
Here Xerxes, when he was retreating from Greece after his defeat in the battle, is said to have built both this palace and the citadel of Celaenae.
Cyrus stayed there thirty days;
and Clearchus the Spartan exile arrived with a thousand hoplites, eight hundred Thracian peltasts and two hundred Cretan archers.
At the same time Sosis the Syracusan arrived too with three hundred hoplites, and Sophaenetus with a thousand Arcadian hoplites.
And there in the park Cyrus held a review and count of the Greeks, and they came to eleven thousand hoplites in all and about two thousand peltasts.
From there he marched two stages, ten parasangs, to Peltae, an inhabited city.
There he stayed three days;
during these Xenias the Arcadian celebrated the Lycaean festival with sacrifices and held games;
the prizes were golden strigils;
Cyrus too watched the games.
From there he marched two stages, twelve parasangs, to Ceramon Agora, an inhabited city, the last one towards Mysia.
From there he marched three stages, thirty parasangs, to Caystrou Pedion, an inhabited city.
There he stayed five days;
the soldiers were owed more than three months' pay, and they often went to his doors and demanded it.
He kept putting them off with talk of hopes and was clearly distressed;
for it was not in Cyrus' character to hold back payment when he had the money.
There Epyaxa, the wife of Syennesis the king of the Cilicians, comes to Cyrus;
and she was said to have given Cyrus a great deal of money.
At any rate Cyrus then paid the army four months' wages.
The Cilician queen had a bodyguard of Cilicians and Aspendians around her;
and it was also said that Cyrus slept with the Cilician queen.
From there he marches two stages, ten parasangs, to Thymbrium, an inhabited city.
There beside the road was a spring called the spring of Midas, the king of the Phrygians, at which Midas is said to have caught the satyr by mixing wine into it.
