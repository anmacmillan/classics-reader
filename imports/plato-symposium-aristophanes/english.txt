Aristophanes took his turn, he said, and began: "It did indeed stop, though not before the sneeze had been applied to it, which makes me wonder whether the orderly part of the body craves such noises and ticklings as a sneeze is;
for it stopped completely the moment I applied the sneeze to it."
And Eryximachus said, "My good Aristophanes, watch what you are doing.
You are playing the buffoon when you are about to speak, and you force me to become a watchman over your speech, in case you say something laughable, when you could have spoken in peace."
And Aristophanes laughed, he said, and replied, "Well said, Eryximachus — let what I have said be unsaid.
But do not keep watch over me; for my fear about what is going to be said is not that I may say things that are laughable — that would be pure gain, and native ground for our Muse — but that they may be ridiculous."
"So you let fly your shot, Aristophanes," he said, "and think you will get away;
no — pay attention, and speak as one who will have to give an account.
Though perhaps, if I see fit, I shall let you off."
"Well then, Eryximachus," said Aristophanes, "I do indeed intend to speak in a different way from the way you and Pausanias spoke.
For it seems to me that human beings have entirely failed to perceive the power of Eros, since if they did perceive it they would have built him the greatest temples and altars, and would offer him the greatest sacrifices — not as now, when none of this is done for him, though of all things it most ought to be done.
For he is the most man-loving of the gods, being a helper of mankind and a healer of those ills whose cure would bring the greatest happiness to the human race.
So I shall try to introduce his power to you, and you shall be teachers of the others.
But first you must learn about human nature and what has happened to it.
For our nature long ago was not the same as it is now, but of a different kind.
In the first place, there were three sexes of human beings, not two as now, male and female, but a third one besides, sharing in both of these — of which the name survives today, though the thing itself has vanished;
for the androgynous was then one sex, both in form and in name compounded of both, the male and the female, whereas now it does not exist except as a name lying about in reproach.
Next, the shape of every human being was entirely round, with back and sides forming a circle; it had four hands, and legs equal in number to the hands, and two faces on a rounded neck, alike in every way;
and one head set on the two faces, which lay in opposite directions, and four ears, and two sets of genitals, and everything else as you might picture from all this.
It walked upright too, as now, in whichever direction it pleased;
and whenever it set off to run fast, then — like tumblers who whirl their legs round into the upright and tumble in a circle — they spun rapidly round, pushing off from their limbs, which were then eight.
And the reason the sexes were three, and of this sort, was that the male was in origin the offspring of the sun, the female of the earth, and the one sharing in both, of the moon — for the moon too shares in both;
and they were round, both they themselves and their manner of moving, because they took after their parents.
Now they were terrible in strength and might, and their ambitions were great, and they made an attempt on the gods; and what Homer tells of Ephialtes and Otus is told of them — that they attempted to make an ascent into heaven, meaning to attack the gods.
So Zeus and the other gods deliberated about what they should do with them, and they were at a loss;
for they could not see how to kill them and wipe out the race with thunderbolts, as they had the giants — for then the honours and sacrifices they received from mankind would vanish too — nor how to let them go on running riot.
At last, and with difficulty, Zeus had an idea and spoke: "I think I have a device," he said, "by which human beings could go on existing and yet cease from their outrageousness, by being made weaker.
For now," he said, "I shall cut each of them in two, and they will be at once weaker and at once more useful to us, through having become more numerous;
and they will walk upright on two legs.
But if they still seem to run riot and refuse to keep the peace, I shall cut them in two again," he said, "so that they will go about hopping on one leg."
Having said this, he began cutting the human beings in two, like men slicing sorb-apples for pickling, or like men slicing eggs with a hair;
and each one he cut, he told Apollo to turn the face and the half of the neck round toward the cut, so that, gazing on his own severing, the human being might be better behaved — and he told him to heal the rest.
And Apollo turned the face round, and, drawing the skin together from every side over what is now called the belly, like a drawstring purse, he made a single opening and tied it off in the middle of the belly — the thing they call the navel.
And he smoothed out the many other wrinkles and shaped the chest, using a tool of the kind shoemakers use when they smooth the wrinkles out of leather on the last;
but he left a few, those around the belly itself and the navel, to be a memorial of what happened long ago.
So when their nature had been cut in two, each half, longing for its own other half, kept coming together with it; and throwing their arms around one another and entwining together, desiring to grow into one, they began dying of hunger and of general idleness, because they refused to do anything apart from each other.
And whenever one of the halves died and the other was left behind, the one left behind would seek out another and entwine with it, whether it met with the half of a whole woman — what we now call a woman — or with the half of a man;
and so they kept perishing.
But Zeus took pity and provided another device: he moved their genitals round to the front — for until then they had these too on the outside, and they begot and gave birth not in one another but in the earth, like cicadas — so he moved them round to the front in this way and thereby made generation take place in one another, through the male in the female, for this purpose: so that in the embrace, if a man met with a woman, they might beget and the race might continue, while at the same time, if male met with male, there might at least be satisfaction from their union, and they might rest, turn to their work, and attend to the rest of life.
So it is from that far back that love of one another has been implanted in human beings — love, the gatherer of our ancient nature, who tries to make one out of two and to heal human nature.
Each of us, then, is a matching half-token of a human being, since we have been cut, like flatfish, two out of one;
and so each of us is forever seeking the half-token that matches himself.
Now all the men who are a slice of the common sex, which was then called androgynous, are lovers of women, and most adulterers have come from this kind, and likewise all the women who are lovers of men and adulteresses come from this kind.
But all the women who are a slice of a woman pay no great attention to men, but rather are turned toward women, and the hetairistriai come from this kind.
And all who are a slice of a male pursue the male, and while they are boys, being little cuts of the male, they love men and delight in lying beside and being entwined with men, and these are the best of boys and youths, since they are the most manly by nature.
Some people, to be sure, say they are shameless, but they lie;
for they do this not out of shamelessness but out of boldness and courage and manliness, welcoming what is like themselves.
And here is strong proof:
for indeed, when they are fully grown, such men alone turn out fit for public affairs.
And when they become men, they love boys and by nature pay no attention to marriage and the begetting of children, but are compelled to it by custom;
rather, it satisfies them to live out their lives with one another unmarried.
In every way, then, such a man becomes a lover of boys and a lover of his lover, always welcoming what is akin to him.
So whenever the lover of boys, or anyone else, actually meets that very one who is his own half, then they are struck with wonder by friendship and kinship and love, and are unwilling, one might almost say, to be parted from each other even for a little time.
And these are the ones who pass their whole lives with one another, yet they could not even say what they want to get from each other.
For no one would suppose that this is the intercourse of sexual pleasure — that it is for the sake of this that the one delights in being with the other with such great earnestness;
no, the soul of each is plainly wanting something else, which it cannot say, but it divines what it wants, and speaks in riddles.
And if Hephaestus, with his tools, were to stand over them as they lay together and ask,
"What is it, you humans, that you want to have from one another?" —
and if, when they were at a loss, he were to ask them again,
"Is this what you desire, to be together in the same place with each other as completely as possible, so as not to leave each other night or day?
For if this is what you desire, I am willing to melt you together and weld you into one, so that from being two you become one, and as long as you live you both live a common life as one, and when you die, there in Hades you are again one instead of two, having died a common death;
but consider whether this is what you long for, and whether it would satisfy you to obtain it" —
on hearing this, we know that not a single one would refuse, nor would anyone be found to want anything else; rather, each would think he had simply heard the very thing he had long desired: to come together and be melted into one with his beloved, and from two to become one.
For this is the cause: that our ancient nature was of this kind and we were whole;
and so the desire and pursuit of the whole is called love.
And before this, as I say, we were one, but now, because of our wrongdoing, we have been settled apart by the god, just as the Arcadians were by the Spartans;
so there is fear that, if we are not orderly toward the gods, we shall be split in two yet again, and shall go about like the figures carved in relief on the gravestones, sawn in half down the line of our noses, having become like split dice.
For these reasons, then, every man must urge every other to be pious toward the gods, so that we may escape the one fate and obtain the other, with Eros as our leader and general.
Let no one act against him — and whoever makes himself hateful to the gods does act against him — for by becoming friends with the god and being reconciled to him, we shall find and meet with the beloveds that are truly our own, which few men nowadays do.
And let Eryximachus not interrupt me, making a comedy of my speech, as though I mean Pausanias and Agathon — for perhaps they too happen to be among these, and are both male by nature — but I for my part am speaking about everyone, both men and women, when I say that our race would become happy in this way: if we brought our love to fulfilment and each of us obtained his own beloved, returning to his ancient nature.
And if this is best, then necessarily what comes nearest to it among the things now possible is best:
and that is to obtain a beloved whose nature is after one's own heart;
and if we sing the praises of the god responsible for this, we would justly sing the praises of Eros, who both in the present benefits us most by leading us to what is our own, and for the future offers the greatest hopes: that if we show piety toward the gods, he will restore us to our ancient nature and, healing us, make us blessed and happy.
This, Eryximachus, he said, is my speech about Eros, a different sort from yours.
So, as I begged you, do not make a comedy of it, so that we may also hear what each of the rest will say — or rather what each of the two will say;
for Agathon and Socrates are the ones left.
Well, I will obey you, he said Eryximachus said;
for indeed I heard your speech with pleasure.
And if I did not know that Socrates and Agathon are masters in matters of love, I would be very afraid that they might be at a loss for words, because so many things of every kind have already been said;
but as it is, I am confident all the same.
