First, then, it is right for me to defend myself, men of Athens, against the first false charges brought against me and my first accusers, and then against the later ones and the later accusers.
For many accusers have come before you against me, long ago now and for many years, saying nothing true, and I fear them more than Anytus and his friends, although these too are dangerous;
but those others are more dangerous, gentlemen, who took hold of most of you from childhood and tried to persuade you and accused me, quite untruly, saying that there is a certain Socrates, a wise man, a thinker about the things in the sky, who has investigated everything beneath the earth and makes the weaker argument the stronger.
These men, men of Athens, who spread this rumour about, are my dangerous accusers;
for those who hear them think that people who investigate these things do not even believe in gods.
Besides, these accusers are many and have been accusing me for a long time already, and what is more they spoke to you at the age when you would most readily believe them, some of you being children and youngsters, simply prosecuting a case that went by default, with nobody to defend.
And the most absurd thing of all is that it is not even possible to know and say their names, except perhaps if one of them happens to be a comic playwright.
All those who, using envy and slander, tried to persuade you— and some, having been persuaded themselves, persuading others— all these are the hardest to deal with;
for it is not possible to bring any of them up here or to refute any one of them, but I simply have to fight with shadows, as it were, in making my defence, and refute with no one answering.
So you too must accept, as I say, that my accusers are of two kinds, some who have accused me just now, others those of long ago whom I am talking about, and you must think that I should defend myself against those first;
for you too heard them accusing me earlier and much more than these later ones.
