Now tell us further, Meletus, in Zeus' name: is it better to live among good citizens or bad ones?
Answer, my friend;
it's not a hard question I'm asking.
Don't bad people do some harm to those who are always nearest to them, and good people some good?
Certainly.
Is there anyone who wants to be harmed by those around him rather than benefited?
Answer, my good man;
the law, after all, requires you to answer.
Is there anyone who wants to be harmed?
Of course not.
Come then: are you bringing me here on the grounds that I corrupt the young and make them worse intentionally or unintentionally?
Intentionally, I say.
What, Meletus?
Are you, young as you are, so much wiser than I am at my age, that you have realised that bad people always do some harm to those closest to them, and good people good, while I have reached such a pitch of ignorance that I don't even know this: that if I make one of my companions wicked, I risk suffering some harm from him, so that I do this great harm intentionally, as you say?
I am not convinced of this by you, Meletus, and I don't think anyone else is either;
either I do not corrupt them, or if I do, I do it unintentionally, so either way you are lying.
And if I corrupt them unintentionally, the law is not to bring people here for such involuntary errors, but to take them aside privately and teach and admonish them;
for clearly, if I learn, I shall stop doing what I do unintentionally.
But you avoided associating with me and teaching me, and were unwilling to, and instead you bring me here, where the law says to bring those who need punishment, not instruction.
But no, men of Athens: what I was saying is now clear, that Meletus has never cared about these things, great or small.
