When I heard this, I thought it over like this:
What on earth does the god mean, and what is his riddle?
For I am aware that I am not wise in anything, great or small;
what then does he mean by declaring that I am the wisest?
For surely he is not lying;
that is not permitted for him.
And for a long time I was at a loss as to what he meant;
then, very reluctantly, I turned to investigating it in some such way as this.
I went to one of those who are reputed to be wise, thinking that there, if anywhere, I would refute the oracle and show the oracle’s answer: “This man is wiser than I am, but you said I was.”
So when I examined this man— I need not mention his name, but he was one of the politicians with whom I had this sort of experience, men of Athens, when I examined him and talked with him— this man seemed to me to be thought wise by many other people and above all by himself, but not to be so;
and then I tried to show him that he thought he was wise, but was not.
As a result I became hated by him and by many of those present;
so as I went away I reasoned to myself that I am wiser than this man;
for it is likely that neither of us knows anything fine and good, but he thinks he knows something when he does not, whereas I, just as I do not know, do not think I know either;
I seem, then, to be wiser than him in just this one small way: that what I do not know, I do not think I know either.
From there I went to another of those reputed to be wiser than he was, and the very same thing seemed to me to be the case, and there I became hated by him and by many others.
After that I went on to one after another, aware, and grieved and afraid, that I was becoming hated, but still it seemed necessary to put the god’s business first— so I had to go, in examining what the oracle meant, to all those who were reputed to know something.
