Still, tell us, Meletus: how do you say I corrupt the young?
Or is it clear, according to the indictment you wrote, that it is by teaching them not to believe in the gods the city believes in, but in other, new spiritual things?
Isn't this what you say, that I corrupt them by teaching this?
Yes, that is emphatically what I say.
Then by these very gods we are now talking about, Meletus, tell me and these gentlemen here still more clearly.
For I cannot make out whether you are saying that I teach people to believe that some gods exist — and so I myself believe that gods exist and am not an atheist at all, and am not guilty in that respect — yet not the ones the city believes in but others, and that this is what you charge me with, that they are others; or whether you are saying that I do not believe in gods at all myself and teach this to others.
That is what I say: that you do not believe in gods at all.
You amazing Meletus, why do you say that?
So I don't even believe that the sun or the moon are gods, as the rest of mankind do?
No, by Zeus, gentlemen of the jury, since he says the sun is a stone and the moon is earth.
Do you think you're accusing Anaxagoras, my dear Meletus?
And do you so despise these men, and think them so illiterate, that they don't know that the books of Anaxagoras of Clazomenae are full of such arguments?
And so the young learn these things from me, when they can buy them now and then for a drachma at most in the orchestra and laugh at Socrates if he pretends they are his own, especially when they are so absurd?
But, in Zeus' name, is that how I seem to you?
That I believe no god exists?
No, by Zeus, not in any way whatsoever.
You are not to be believed, Meletus, not even, it seems to me, by yourself.
For this man seems to me, men of Athens, thoroughly insolent and unrestrained, and to have written this indictment simply out of a kind of insolence, lack of restraint and youthful rashness.
