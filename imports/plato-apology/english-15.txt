That would be terrible, and then someone really could justly bring me into court, for not believing that gods exist, by disobeying the oracle and fearing death and thinking I was wise when I am not.
For to fear death, gentlemen, is nothing other than to seem wise when one is not;
for it is to seem to know what one does not know.
For no one knows death, not even whether it happens to be the greatest of all goods for a human being, yet people fear it as though they knew well that it is the greatest of evils.
And yet how is this not ignorance, the shameful kind, thinking one knows what one does not know?
But I, gentlemen, differ from most people in this respect too, perhaps, and if I were to claim to be wiser than anyone in anything, it would be in this: that not knowing enough about things in Hades, I also think that I do not know;
but that doing wrong and disobeying one's better, whether god or man, is bad and shameful, that I do know.
So rather than the evils that I know to be evils, I will never fear or flee things that I do not know may not in fact be good;
so even if you now acquit me, disbelieving Anytus, who said that either I should not have been brought here at all or, once I had been, it was impossible not to put me to death, telling you that if I got off your sons would at once practise what Socrates teaches and would all be utterly corrupted — if in reply to this you were to say to me:
“Socrates, this time we will not listen to Anytus but we acquit you, on this condition, however: that you no longer spend your time in this search or do philosophy;
and if you are caught still doing it, you will die” — if, as I said, you were to acquit me on these terms, I would say to you: “Men of Athens, I respect and love you, but I will obey the god rather than you, and as long as I draw breath and am able, I shall never stop doing philosophy and exhorting you and pointing things out to whichever of you I happen to meet, saying the sort of thing I usually say: ‘My excellent friend, you are an Athenian, a citizen of the greatest city, the most renowned for wisdom and power; are you not ashamed of caring about how to get as much money as possible, and reputation and honour, while you do not care or give any thought to wisdom and truth and how your soul may be as good as possible?’”
And if one of you disputes this and says he does care, I will not let him go at once or walk away, but I will question him and examine him and refute him, and if he does not seem to me to possess virtue, though he says he does, I will reproach him for treating what is worth most as least important, and what is inferior as more important.
I will do this to whoever I meet, young or old, foreigner or citizen, but especially to the citizens, since you are closer to me by birth.
For this is what the god commands, be sure of it, and I believe that no greater good has ever come to you in the city than my service to the god.
For I go around doing nothing else than persuading you, young and old, not to care for your bodies or your money before, or as intensely as, for your soul, how it may be as good as possible, telling you that virtue does not come from money, but that from virtue come money and all the other good things for people, both in private and in public.
