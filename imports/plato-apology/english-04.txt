For each of these, gentlemen, can go into any city and persuade the young— who can associate free of charge with any of their own fellow citizens they like— to leave the company of those, to associate with them instead, paying money, and to be grateful into the bargain.
Indeed, there is another wise man here, from Paros, who I learned was visiting the city;
for I happened to meet a man who has paid more money to sophists than all the others put together, Callias son of Hipponicus;
so I asked him— for he has two sons— “Callias,” I said, “if your two sons had been colts or calves, we could have found and hired a supervisor for them who would make them fine and good in the excellence proper to them; he would be some horse-trainer or farmer;
but now, since they are human beings, whom do you have in mind to take as their supervisor?
Who has knowledge of this kind of excellence, the excellence of a human being and a citizen?
For I suppose you have looked into it, since you have sons.
Is there someone,” I said, “or not?”
“Certainly,” he said.
“Who,” I said, “and where is he from, and how much does he charge for teaching?”
“Evenus, Socrates,” he said, “from Paros, five minas.”
And I thought Evenus a lucky man, if he really has this skill and teaches it at such a modest fee.
I, at any rate, would be preening myself and putting on airs if I knew these things;
but the fact is I do not know them, men of Athens.
Perhaps, then, one of you might reply:
“But Socrates, what is this business of yours?
Where have these slanders against you come from?
For surely, if you were not busying yourself with anything more out of the ordinary than other people, such a rumour and reputation would not have arisen, unless you were doing something different from most people.
So tell us what it is, so that we do not make up our own minds about you off the cuff.”
The person saying this seems to me to speak fairly, and I will try to show you what it is that has given me this name and this slander.
