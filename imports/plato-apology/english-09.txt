As for the charges my first accusers brought, let this be a sufficient defence before you;
next I shall try to defend myself against Meletus, that good man and lover of his city, as he says, and against the later accusers.
Once again, then, as though these were a different set of accusers, let us take up their sworn indictment in its turn.
It runs something like this:
Socrates, it says, is guilty of corrupting the young and of not believing in the gods the city believes in, but in other, new spiritual things.
Such is the charge;
let us examine each point of this charge in turn.
He says, then, that I am guilty of corrupting the young.
But I, men of Athens, say that Meletus is guilty, because he jokes about serious matters, lightly bringing people to trial and pretending to be serious and concerned about things he has never cared about at all;
That this is so, I shall try to show you too.
Come here, Meletus, and tell me:
Isn't it true that you consider it of the greatest importance that the young should be as good as possible?
I do.
Come then, tell these men: who makes them better?
Clearly you know, since it matters to you.
For you have found the one who corrupts them, as you say — me — and you bring me before these men and accuse me;
so come, tell them who makes them better, and point him out.
— You see, Meletus, that you are silent and have nothing to say?
Yet doesn't that seem shameful to you, and sufficient proof of what I say, that you have never cared about it at all?
But tell me, my dear fellow, who makes them better?
The laws.
But that is not what I'm asking, my good man: I'm asking which person, who knows first of all this very thing, the laws.
These men, Socrates: the jurors.
What do you mean, Meletus?
These men are able to educate the young and make them better?
Certainly.
All of them, or some of them and not others?
All of them.
Well said, by Hera: what an abundance of benefactors!
