How you, men of Athens, have been affected by my accusers, I do not know;
but I myself, at any rate, almost forgot who I was because of them, so persuasively did they speak.
And yet, practically speaking, they have said nothing true at all.
But of their many lies one amazed me most: the one where they said that you had to be careful not to be deceived by me, since I am such a clever speaker.
For not to be ashamed that they will be refuted by me in fact straight away, as soon as I turn out to be no clever speaker in any way whatever, that seemed to me the most shameless thing about them, unless of course by a clever speaker these people mean the one who speaks the truth;
for if that is what they mean, I would admit that I am an orator, though not after their fashion.
Well then, these men, as I say, have said little or nothing true, but from me you will hear the whole truth— not, by Zeus, men of Athens, in finely phrased speeches like theirs, dressed up with fancy words and phrases, but you will hear things said at random in whatever words come to hand— for I trust that what I say is just— and let none of you expect anything else;
for surely it would not be fitting, gentlemen, for a man of my age to come before you like a youngster making up speeches.
And yet I do very much ask and beg this of you, men of Athens:
if you hear me defending myself with the same words I usually speak in the marketplace at the bankers’ tables, where many of you have heard me, and elsewhere, do not be surprised or make an uproar because of that.
For this is how things stand.
Now for the first time I have come up before a court, at the age of seventy;
so I am simply a stranger to the way of speaking here.
So just as, if I really happened to be a foreigner, you would surely forgive me if I spoke in the dialect and manner in which I had been brought up, so now too I ask this of you as something just, as it seems to me at least: to let my manner of speaking be— for it may be worse, it may be better— and to consider this alone and pay attention to this: whether what I say is just or not;
for that is the virtue of a juror, while an orator’s is to speak the truth.
