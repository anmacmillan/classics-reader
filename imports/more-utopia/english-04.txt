“But you have gone very far astray,” he said; “he has sailed, indeed, not like Palinurus, but like Ulysses; or rather, like Plato.
This Raphael — for so he is called, Hythlodaeus by family name — is not unlearned in Latin and most learned in Greek — which he studied more keenly than Roman letters because he had given himself wholly to philosophy, and in that field he found that nothing of any importance survives in Latin except some works of Seneca and Cicero —
and having left to his brothers the inheritance he had at home — for he is Portuguese — out of a desire to see the world he joined Amerigo Vespucci, and on the last three of those four voyages that are now read everywhere he was his constant companion, except that on the last he did not come back with him.
For he contrived, indeed he wrung it from Amerigo, that he should himself be one of the twenty-four who were left in a fort at the farthest point of the last voyage.
So he was left behind, that his wish might be humoured, since he cared more for travel than for a tomb.
For these sayings are constantly on his lips: ‘He who has no urn is covered by the sky’, and ‘From everywhere the road to the gods is the same length.’
And this frame of mind, had God not been gracious to him, would have cost him dear.
However, after Vespucci's departure he travelled through many regions with five Castilian companions, and at last, by wonderful fortune carried to Taprobane, he came from there to Calicut, where he conveniently found Portuguese ships and was at length carried back, beyond all hope, to his homeland.”
When Peter had told me this, I thanked him for being so obliging to me, that he had taken such pains for me to enjoy the talk of a man whose conversation he hoped I would welcome, and I turned to Raphael; then, when we had greeted each other and said those commonplaces usually said at a first meeting of strangers, we went off to my house, and there, sitting in the garden on a bench laid with grassy turf, we talked together.
