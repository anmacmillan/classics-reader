A high, broad wall surrounds the town, set thick with towers and bulwarks; a dry ditch, but deep and wide and choked with thorn hedges, encircles the ramparts on three sides; on the fourth the river itself serves as a ditch.
The streets are well laid out, both for traffic and against the winds; the buildings are handsome and by no means mean, and they are seen in a long, unbroken row running the whole length of each block, the house-fronts facing each other.
These fronts of the blocks are divided by a street twenty feet wide.
Behind the houses, along the whole length of the block, lies a garden, broad and enclosed on every side by the backs of the blocks.
There is no house that does not have a front door on to the street and a back door into the garden as well.
What is more, the double doors, which open with an easy push of the hand and then close again of their own accord, let anyone in: so nothing anywhere is private.
For every ten years they exchange the very houses by lot.
They set great store by these gardens.
In them they have vines, fruit, herbs and flowers,
kept with such brightness and care that I have never seen anything more fruitful anywhere, nothing more elegant.
Their zeal in this is kindled not only by the pleasure itself but also by the rivalry between the blocks over the tending of each one's garden.
And certainly you would hardly find anything in the whole city better suited either to the citizens' use or to their pleasure.
And so it seems that its founder cared for nothing more than for gardens of this kind.
