When Raphael had recounted these things, although not a few things occurred to me that seemed to have been quite absurdly established in the customs and laws of that people — not only in their method of waging war, their rites and religion, and other institutions of theirs besides, but above all in the very thing that is the chief foundation of their whole system, namely their common life and livelihood without any traffic in money, by which one thing all nobility, magnificence, splendour and majesty, the true glories and ornaments of a commonwealth in the common opinion, are utterly overthrown,
yet because I knew he was tired from talking, and was not quite sure whether he could bear to have views held contrary to his own, especially as I remembered that he had censured certain people on that very score, as if they feared they would not be thought wise enough unless they found something for which to pick at the discoveries of others,
I therefore praised both their institutions and his speech, took him by the hand and led him in to supper, saying first, however, that there would be another time to think more deeply about these same matters and to talk them over with him more fully; and I wish that might one day come about.
Meanwhile, while I cannot agree with everything that was said, though it came from a man beyond dispute most learned and at the same time most experienced in human affairs, I readily confess that there are very many things in the Utopian commonwealth which I would rather wish for than hope for in our cities.
