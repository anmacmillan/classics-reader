The chief and almost the only business of the syphogrants is to take care and see to it that no one sits about idle,
but that each applies himself diligently to his own craft, yet without being worn out by unbroken toil from early morning till late at night, like a beast of burden.
For that is a hardship worse than slavery, and yet almost everywhere it is the life of working men, except among the Utopians, who divide the day, counting in the night, into twenty-four equal hours and assign only six to work: three before noon, after which they go to lunch; and after lunch, when they have rested for two afternoon hours, three more given again to labour, which they close with supper.
Since they count the first hour from noon, they go to bed about the eighth hour.
Sleep claims eight hours.
Whatever time lies between the hours of work, sleep and food is left to each person's own discretion, not so that he may squander it in luxury or sloth, but so that, freed from his trade, he may spend it well, as his mind inclines, on some other pursuit.
Most of them devote these intervals to letters.
For it is the custom that public lectures are held every day in the hours before dawn, which only those are obliged to attend who have been expressly chosen for learning.
But from every rank a very great crowd, men and women alike, flocks to hear the lectures, some to one, some to another, as each one's nature inclines.
Yet if anyone would rather spend this very time on his craft, as happens with many — whose minds do not rise to the contemplation of any branch of learning — he is not prevented; indeed he is even praised, as useful to the commonwealth.
