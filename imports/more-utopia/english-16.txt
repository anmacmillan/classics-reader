To meet these difficulties they have devised a certain plan which, while it agrees with the rest of their institutions, is utterly at odds with ours — among whom gold is so highly prized and so carefully hidden away — and is therefore incredible except to those who have seen it.
For while they eat and drink from vessels of earthenware and glass, most elegant indeed, but cheap all the same,
from gold and silver, not only in the common halls but in private houses too, they make chamber pots everywhere and all the meanest vessels.
Besides this, the chains and heavy fetters with which they restrain their slaves are wrought from the same metals.
Finally, anyone whom some crime has made infamous has gold rings hanging from his ears, gold encircles his fingers, a gold collar goes round his neck, and at last his very head is bound with gold.
Thus they take care by every means that gold and silver are held in disgrace among them; and so it comes about that these metals, which other nations let be torn from them with almost as much pain as their own entrails, among the Utopians, if ever circumstances required them all to be carried off at once, would leave no one thinking he had lost a single penny.
They also gather pearls on the shores, and indeed diamonds and carbuncles on certain rocks; yet they do not go looking for them, but polish those that chance puts in their way.
With these they adorn their little children, who, just as in the first years of childhood they glory and preen themselves in such ornaments, so, when they have grown a little older and notice that only children use such trifles, lay them aside with no prompting from their parents, out of their own sense of shame.
Just as our children, when they grow up, throw away their nuts, lockets and dolls.
