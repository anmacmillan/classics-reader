Moreover, as the story goes, and as the very look of the place makes plain, that land was once not surrounded by the sea.
But Utopus, whose name the island bears as its conqueror (for before that time it was called Abraxa), and who brought a rude and rustic mob to that level of culture and humanity in which it now surpasses almost all other mortals, won the victory at his very first landing, had fifteen miles cut through on the side where the land was joined to the continent, and led the sea around the land.
And since he had compelled not only the inhabitants to this work — so that they should not regard the labour as a disgrace — but also added all his own soldiers besides, the work was shared among so great a multitude of men and finished with incredible speed, so that it struck the neighbours — who at first had laughed at the folly of the undertaking — with wonder at its success and with terror.
The island has fifty-four cities, all spacious and magnificent, entirely identical in language, customs, institutions and laws; all have the same layout, and everywhere, as far as the site allows, the same appearance.
Those of them that lie nearest to one another are twenty-four miles apart.
Again, none is so remote that one cannot reach another city from it on foot in a single day's journey.
From each city three citizens, old and experienced in affairs, meet every year at Amaurotum to discuss the common business of the island.
For that city — because, situated as it were at the navel of the land, it lies most conveniently for the envoys of all parts — is held to be the first and chief.
