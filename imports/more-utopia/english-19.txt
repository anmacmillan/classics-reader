He shouted that they were profane, and their followers impious and sacrilegious, to be punished in everlasting fire.
When he had been preaching like this for a long time, they arrested him and tried him — not for contempt of religion but for stirring up a riot among the people — and, having convicted him, punished him with exile; for they count this among their most ancient institutions, that no one should be harmed for his religion.
For Utopus, right from the very beginning, having learned that before his arrival the inhabitants had been constantly fighting among themselves about religion, and having noticed that this very thing — that, disunited in the common cause, the separate sects fought each on its own for their country — had given him the opportunity of conquering them all,
once he had won the victory, decreed first of all that each person might follow whatever religion he pleased, and that he might strive to bring others over to his own only so far as to support it quietly and modestly with reasons, not to tear the others down bitterly if he did not persuade by his arguments, and that he should use no force and refrain from abuse; anyone who contends too insolently on this matter they punish with exile or slavery.
Utopus established this not only out of regard for peace, which he saw utterly destroyed by constant strife and implacable hatred, but because he thought that such a decree was in the interest of religion itself, about which he dared not lay down anything rashly, being uncertain whether God, desiring varied and manifold worship, inspires one thing in one man and another in another; but to exact by force and threats that what you believe true should seem the same to everyone, that he certainly judged both arrogant and foolish.
Then, even if one religion alone is true and all the others vain, he readily foresaw — provided the matter were handled with reason and moderation — that in the end the force of truth would of itself emerge at some point and stand out.
But if the contest were waged with arms and riots, since the worst men are the most obstinate, the best and holiest religion would be smothered among the emptiest superstitions, like corn among thorns and brambles.
So he left this whole matter open, and left each person free to believe what he thought should be believed.
