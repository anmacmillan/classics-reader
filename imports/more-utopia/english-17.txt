And so how far these institutions, so unlike those of other nations, breed dispositions of mind just as unlike, never became as plain to me as in the case of the Anemolian ambassadors.
They came to Amaurotum — while I was there — and because they were coming to negotiate on matters of great weight, those three citizens from each city had arrived ahead of them; but all the ambassadors of the neighbouring nations who had landed there before, to whom the customs of the Utopians were well known, who understood that no honour was paid there to sumptuous dress, and knew that silk was held in contempt and gold was even a mark of infamy, had made it their custom to come in the plainest attire they could.
But the Anemolians, because they lived further off and had had fewer dealings with them, on hearing that they all went about in the same dress, and a rough one at that, were persuaded that the Utopians did not have what they did not use; and being themselves more proud than wise, they resolved by the elegance of their apparel to represent some kind of gods, and to dazzle the eyes of the wretched Utopians with the splendour of their finery.
And so the three ambassadors made their entry with a hundred attendants, all in parti-coloured clothing, most of them in silk; the ambassadors themselves — for at home they were noblemen — in cloth of gold, with great chains and gold earrings, gold rings on their hands besides, and strings of jewels hanging from their caps as well, which glittered with pearls and gems; decked out, in short, with all the things that among the Utopians were either the punishments of slaves, the marks of disgrace of the infamous, or the toys of children.
