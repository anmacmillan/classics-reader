Whoever knows one of the cities will know them all, so entirely alike are they — as far as the nature of the site does not prevent it.
So I shall describe one of them — nor does it much matter which — but which rather than Amaurotum?
For none is more worthy, since the others yield it precedence for the sake of the senate, and none is better known to me, as I lived there for five whole years.
Amaurotum, then, lies on the gentle slope of a hill, almost square in shape.
For its breadth, beginning a little below the summit of the hill, runs for two miles down to the river Anydrus; along the bank it is somewhat longer.
The Anydrus rises eighty miles above Amaurotum from a modest spring, but, swollen by the inflow of other rivers, two of them of middling size, it spreads to five hundred paces wide before the city itself, and soon, wider still, after flowing on for sixty miles, it is received by the ocean.
Over this whole stretch that lies between the city and the sea, and for some miles above the city too, the tide flows in for six hours on end and then ebbs, alternating in a swift current.
When the sea comes in, it fills the whole bed of the Anydrus with its waves for thirty miles in length, driving the river back.
Then for some distance further it spoils the river's water with brine; after that the river, gradually growing sweet, glides clean through the city, and in its turn, pure and unspoilt, follows the retreating sea almost to the very mouth.
The city is joined to the opposite bank of the river by a bridge, not of wooden piers and piles but of stonework, splendidly arched, at the part that lies furthest from the sea, so that ships can sail past the whole of that side of the city unhindered.
