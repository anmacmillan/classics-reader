“And in this way it comes about that in many places corn is far dearer.
Indeed the price of wool too has risen so much that the poorer sort, who among you usually make cloth from it, cannot buy it at all, and for that reason more men are sent away from work into idleness.
For after the pastures were enlarged a murrain carried off a vast number of sheep, as if God were avenging their greed by sending a plague upon the sheep which it would have been juster to hurl upon the owners' own heads.
But however much the number of sheep increases, the price does not fall one bit.
For the trade in them, if it cannot be called a monopoly because it is not one man who sells, is certainly an oligopoly.
For they have fallen almost wholly into the hands of a few, and those the same rich men, whom no need compels to sell before they please, and they do not please until they may sell for as much as they please.
Now the other kinds of livestock are equally dear for the same reason, and all the more because, with farmsteads pulled down and husbandry in decline, there is no one to see to the breeding.
For those rich men do not rear the young of cattle as they do of sheep; instead they buy them lean and cheap elsewhere, and once they have grown fat on their own pastures, sell them again at a high price.
And so, as I think, the full harm of this business is not yet felt.
For so far they make them dear only in the places where they sell them.
But when for some time they have taken them away from there faster than they can be bred, then at last, as the supply dwindles there too where they are bought up, this country must needs suffer a great scarcity.
Thus the very thing in which this island of yours seemed most fortunate has now been turned to ruin by the shameless greed of a few.
For this dearness of food is the reason why everyone dismisses as many as he can from his household — to go where, pray, but begging, or, as you would more easily persuade men of spirit, robbing?”
