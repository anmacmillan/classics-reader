At that time I owed much to the most reverend father John Morton, Archbishop of Canterbury and Cardinal, and then also Chancellor of England, a man, my dear Peter — for I am going to tell what More knows — venerable no more for his authority than for his wisdom and virtue.
For he was of middling stature, and did not bow to his age, though it was advanced.
His face was one to revere, not to dread.
In meeting he was not difficult.
Yet serious and grave.
He liked at times to test petitioners by addressing them rather sharply, but without harm, to see what wit and what presence of mind each displayed; in this quality, as one akin to his own, he took delight, provided impudence was absent, and embraced it as fit for public affairs; his speech was polished and effective, his knowledge of the law great, his intellect incomparable, his memory excellent to the point of the prodigious.
For these outstanding gifts of nature he had advanced by learning and practice.
The king seemed to trust his counsels very greatly, and the commonwealth to lean on him much — while I was there.
For, having been thrust from school into the court almost from his earliest youth, engaged in the greatest affairs all his life, and constantly tossed on the shifting tides of fortune, he had learned practical wisdom — which, received in this way, does not easily slip away — through many great dangers.
By chance, one day when I was at his table, there was present a certain layman learned in your laws, who, having found an occasion I know not where, began to praise at length that rigorous justice which was then exercised there against thieves, who, he reported, were hanged here and there, sometimes twenty on one gallows; and he said he wondered all the more, since so few escaped punishment, by what evil fate it came about that so many were nevertheless out robbing everywhere.
