As for the Greeks at Thermopylae, first the seer Megistias, looking into the sacrifices, told them of the death that would come to them at dawn; and then there were also deserters who brought news of the Persians' march around the mountain.
These gave the signal while it was still night, and the third were the day-watchers, who ran down from the heights just as the day was breaking.
Then the Greeks took counsel, and their opinions were divided;
for some would not allow them to leave their post, while others argued against this.
After this they parted: some went away, scattered and turned homeward each to his own city, while others prepared to stay there with Leonidas.
It is also said that Leonidas himself sent them away, out of concern that they should not perish;
but for himself and the Spartiates who were present it was not seemly to desert the post they had come to guard in the first place.
I myself incline most strongly to this view: that Leonidas, when he perceived that the allies were unwilling and did not want to share the danger, ordered them to depart, but that for himself it was not honourable to go away;
if he stayed there, great glory would be left behind him, and the prosperity of Sparta would not be blotted out.
For the Spartans had been told by the Pythia, when they consulted the oracle about this war right at its outbreak, that either Lacedaemon would be laid waste by the barbarians or their king would perish.
She gave them this oracle in hexameter verses, speaking as follows.
For you, O dwellers in wide-spaced Sparta, either your great and glorious city is sacked by men of Perseus' line, or, if not that, the land of Lacedaemon shall mourn a dead king of the race of Heracles.
For neither the strength of bulls nor of lions shall hold him in check, meeting him face to face;
for he has the might of Zeus;
and I say he shall not be checked until he has torn one of these two utterly apart.
So Leonidas, reflecting on this and wishing to lay up glory for the Spartiates alone, sent the allies away, rather than that those who left went off in such disorder because they disagreed.
And to me this too is no small proof of the matter: the seer who accompanied this army, Megistias the Acarnanian, said to be descended from Melampus, who had told them from the sacrifices what was about to befall them, was clearly sent away by Leonidas, so that he should not perish with them.
But though he was sent away, he did not leave himself; instead he sent away his son, who was serving with him and was his only child.
So the allies who were sent away went off and obeyed Leonidas, and only the Thespians and Thebans stayed behind with the Lacedaemonians.
Of these the Thebans stayed against their will and unwillingly;
for Leonidas kept them back, treating them as hostages;
but the Thespians stayed most willingly: they refused to go away and abandon Leonidas and those with him, but stayed behind and died with them.
Their commander was Demophilus son of Diadromes.
