When the Lydians arrived and delivered their message, the Pythia is said to have spoken as follows.
“The fate that has been destined is impossible to escape, even for a god.
Croesus has paid for the crime of his ancestor five generations back, who, while he was a bodyguard of the Heraclids, followed a woman's trickery, killed his master and took his royal honour, which in no way belonged to him.
And although Loxias was eager that the disaster of Sardis should fall in the time of Croesus' sons and not in that of Croesus himself, it was not possible to turn aside the Fates.
But as much as they gave way, he achieved and granted to him as a favour.
For he put off the capture of Sardis by three years, and let Croesus know this: that he was captured these three years later than was fated.
Secondly, he came to his aid when he was burning.
As for the oracle that was given, Croesus finds fault with it unjustly.
For Loxias foretold to him that if he made war on the Persians, he would destroy a great empire.
In the face of this, if he meant to take good counsel, he ought to have sent and asked whether the god meant his own empire or that of Cyrus.
But since he did not understand what was said and did not ask again, let him declare himself to blame.
And when he consulted the oracle for the last time, Loxias spoke to him about a mule, and he did not understand this either.
For this Cyrus was indeed a mule:
he was born of two parents not of the same race, a mother of nobler stock and a father of lesser;
for she was a Mede and the daughter of Astyages, king of the Medes, while he was a Persian, a subject of the Medes, and though inferior in every respect he was married to his own mistress.”
This was the answer the Pythia gave to the Lydians, and they brought it back to Sardis and reported it to Croesus.
When he heard it, he acknowledged that the fault was his own and not the god's.
So it went with the empire of Croesus and the first subjugation of Ionia.
