Such were the Lacedaemonians and the Thespians, yet the bravest man of all, it is said, was the Spartiate Dieneces.
They say that he spoke the following words before they engaged the Medes, when he had learned from one of the Trachinians that whenever the barbarians shot their arrows, they hid the sun with the multitude of their shafts:
so great was their multitude.
But he, not at all dismayed by this and making light of the multitude of the Medes, said that the Trachinian stranger brought them nothing but good news, since if the Medes hid the sun, they would fight against them in the shade and not in the sun.
These and other sayings of the same kind, they say, Dieneces the Lacedaemonian left behind as memorials of himself.
After him, it is said, the bravest were two Lacedaemonian brothers, Alpheus and Maron, the sons of Orsiphantus.
Of the Thespians the one who won most renown was a man named Dithyrambus, son of Harmatides.
They were buried just where they fell, and for them, and for those who had died before the others were sent away by Leonidas and departed, an inscription was set up which says this:
Here once four thousand from the Peloponnese fought against three million.
This inscription is for them all, but the Spartiates have one of their own:
Stranger, tell the Lacedaemonians that here we lie, obedient to their words.
That is for the Lacedaemonians, and this is for the seer:
This is the memorial of famous Megistias, whom once the Medes slew when they had crossed the river Spercheius, a seer who, though he knew clearly that the fates of death were coming upon him, could not bring himself to desert the leader of Sparta.
It was the Amphictyons who honoured them with the inscriptions and the pillars, except for the epitaph of the seer;
the inscription for the seer Megistias was written by Simonides, son of Leoprepes, for friendship's sake.
