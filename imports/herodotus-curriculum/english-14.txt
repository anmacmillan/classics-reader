This is what the path is like:
it begins from the river Asopus, which flows through the gorge, and the mountain and the path bear the same name, Anopaea;
this Anopaea runs along the ridge of the mountain and ends at the town of Alpenus, the first of the Locrian towns on the Malian side, and at the rock called Black-Buttock and the Seats of the Cercopes, where it is also at its narrowest.
Along this path, such as I have described it, the Persians crossed the Asopus and marched the whole night, with the mountains of the Oetaeans on their right and those of the Trachinians on their left.
Dawn was breaking when they reached the summit of the mountain.
At this part of the mountain, as I have said before, a thousand Phocian hoplites were on guard, protecting their own country and keeping watch over the path.
For the pass below was guarded by those I have mentioned;
and the path over the mountain was guarded by the Phocians, who had volunteered to Leonidas to take it on.
The Phocians learned that the Persians had come up in this way:
the Persians had made the ascent unseen, since the whole mountain was covered with oak trees.
There was no wind, and a great noise arose, as was natural with leaves strewn underfoot; the Phocians sprang up and began putting on their armour, and at once the barbarians were upon them.
When the Persians saw men putting on armour, they were astonished;
for they had expected that no one would appear to oppose them, and they had run into an army.
Then Hydarnes, terrified that the Phocians might be Lacedaemonians, asked Ephialtes what country the troops came from; and when he had learned the truth exactly, he drew up the Persians for battle.
The Phocians, as they were struck by the many thick-flying arrows, fled away to the peak of the mountain, supposing that the attack had been aimed at them from the start, and they made ready to die.
That was what they were thinking; but the Persians with Ephialtes and Hydarnes paid no attention to the Phocians and went down the mountain with all speed.
