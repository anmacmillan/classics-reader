Then, the Lydians say, Croesus learned of Cyrus's change of mind, and when he saw every man trying to put out the fire but no longer able to get it under control, he cried out loudly to Apollo, calling on him, if any gift of his had ever pleased the god, to stand by him and rescue him from the present evil.
As he called on the god in tears, suddenly clouds gathered out of a clear and windless sky, a storm broke, rain poured down in torrents, and the pyre was put out.
So Cyrus, realising that Croesus was both dear to the gods and a good man, had him brought down from the pyre and asked him the following.
“Croesus, who among men persuaded you to march against my land and become my enemy instead of my friend?”
He replied, “O king, I did this to your good fortune and to my own misfortune; the one to blame for it is the god of the Greeks, who urged me on to make war.
For no one is so foolish as to choose war rather than peace:
in peace sons bury their fathers, but in war fathers bury their sons.
But it was, I suppose, the pleasure of the gods that things should turn out this way.”
So he spoke; Cyrus freed him, seated him near himself and treated him with great respect, and both he and all those around him looked at him with wonder.
But Croesus, lost in thought, kept silent;
then, turning round and seeing the Persians plundering the city of the Lydians, he said, “O king, should I tell you what is on my mind, or ought I to keep silent for now?”
Cyrus told him to take courage and say whatever he wished.
So Croesus asked him, “What is this great crowd doing so eagerly?”
Cyrus said, “It is plundering your city and carrying off your wealth.”
Croesus replied, “It is neither my city nor my wealth they are plundering;
none of it belongs to me any longer.
It is your property they are carrying off and driving away.”
