Yet with remarkable rivalry among the leading men, thanksgivings were decreed at all the shrines, and it was decreed that the Quinquatrus, on which the plot had been uncovered, should be celebrated with annual games; that a golden statue of Minerva should be set up in the senate house and next to it an image of the emperor; and that Agrippina's birthday should be counted among the ill-omened days.
Thrasea Paetus, who had been accustomed to let earlier flatteries pass in silence or with brief assent, walked out of the senate on that occasion, and so created a source of danger for himself without giving the rest a beginning of freedom.
Frequent and meaningless portents also occurred: a woman gave birth to a snake, another was killed by a thunderbolt while in bed with her husband; then the sun was suddenly darkened and all fourteen districts of the city were struck by lightning.
These happened so entirely without the gods' concern that Nero went on with his reign and his crimes for many years afterwards.
Moreover, to deepen the hatred for his mother and to show that his own clemency had increased once she was removed, he restored to their ancestral homes the distinguished women Junia and Calpurnia and the former praetors Valerius Capito and Licinius Gabolus, whom Agrippina had once driven into exile.
He also allowed the ashes of Lollia Paulina to be brought back and a tomb to be built; and he released from punishment Iturius and Calvisius, whom he himself had recently banished.
For Silana had died, having returned to Tarentum from her distant exile when Agrippina, whose enmity had brought her down, was already tottering, or had softened.
Yet he lingered in the towns of Campania, anxious about how he should enter the city and whether he would find the senate compliant and the people enthusiastic: on the other side all the worst men, and no court was ever richer in them, argued that Agrippina's name was hated and that the people's favour had been kindled by her death: he should go without fear and experience in person the reverence felt for him; at the same time they demanded to go on ahead.
And they found things even more eager than they had promised: the tribes coming out to meet him, the senate in festive dress, columns of wives and children arranged by sex and age, and tiers of seats built along his route, as when triumphs are watched.
From there, proud and victorious over the nation's servility, he went up to the Capitol, paid his thanks, and plunged into all the excesses which, though poorly restrained, some sort of respect for his mother had so far held back.
