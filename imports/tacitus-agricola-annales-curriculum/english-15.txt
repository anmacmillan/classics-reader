The gods provided a night bright with stars and calm on a peaceful sea, as if to expose the crime.
The ship had not gone far, with two of her intimates accompanying Agrippina, of whom Crepereius Gallus was standing not far from the helm, while Acerronia, leaning over the feet of her reclining mistress, was joyfully recalling the son's repentance and the mother's recovered influence, when at a given signal the roof of the cabin, weighted with a great deal of lead, collapsed, and Crepereius was crushed and killed instantly: Agrippina and Acerronia were protected by the raised sides of the couch, which happened to be too strong to give way under the weight.
Nor did the break-up of the ship follow, since everyone was in confusion and most, knowing nothing, were even hindering those in the plot.
Then the rowers decided to lean over to one side and so sink the ship: but they could not quickly agree among themselves on a sudden action, and others, by pushing the opposite way, allowed a gentler fall into the sea.
But Acerronia, while she foolishly kept shouting that she was Agrippina and that they should help the emperor's mother, was finished off with poles and oars and whatever ship's gear chance put to hand: Agrippina kept silent and so was less recognised (though she did take one wound on the shoulder); she swam, and then, picked up by some small boats, was carried into the Lucrine lake and brought to her own villa.
There, reflecting that she had been summoned by a deceitful letter and treated with special honour for this very purpose, and that close to the shore a ship, neither driven by winds nor dashed on rocks, had collapsed from its upper part like a contraption on land; noting too Acerronia's killing, and at the same time looking at her own wound, she judged that the only remedy against the plot was not to let it be seen through; and she sent her freedman Agerinus to report to her son that by the kindness of the gods and his good fortune she had escaped a serious accident; she begged him, however alarmed he was by his mother's danger, to put off his wish to visit her; for the present she needed rest.
Meanwhile, feigning unconcern, she had remedies applied to her wound and poultices to her body; she ordered Acerronia's will to be looked for and her property sealed: this alone was no pretence.
