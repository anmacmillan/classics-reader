'Or do you believe that the Romans have as much courage in war as they have wantonness in peace?
Famous through our quarrels and discords, they turn the faults of their enemies into glory for their own army; an army scraped together from the most diverse peoples, which success holds together and disaster will break apart: unless you think that Gauls and Germans and (shameful to say) most of the Britons, though they lend their blood to a foreign tyranny, yet were its enemies longer than its slaves, are held by loyalty and affection.
Fear and terror are weak bonds of affection; remove them, and those who have stopped fearing will begin to hate.
Every incentive to victory is on our side: no wives fire the Romans' courage, no parents are there to reproach them if they flee; most of them have no fatherland, or a different one.
Few in number, panicked by ignorance, staring round at the very sky and sea and forests, all of them unknown, shut in and in a way bound, the gods have delivered them to us.
Do not let an empty show frighten you, or the gleam of gold and silver, which neither protects nor wounds.
In the enemy's own battle line we shall find our forces: the Britons will recognise their own cause, the Gauls will remember their former freedom, the rest of the Germans will desert them just as the Usipi recently abandoned them.
And there is nothing more to fear beyond that: empty forts, colonies of old men, towns sick and at odds between subjects who obey badly and rulers who govern unjustly.
Here is a general, here an army: there lie tribute and mines and all the other punishments of slaves, and whether to endure them for ever or to avenge them at once is decided on this field.
So as you go into battle, think of your ancestors and of your descendants.'
