But to Nero, as he waited for news that the deed was done, word was brought that she had escaped, wounded by a light blow, and had come just close enough to danger to leave no doubt who was behind it.
Then, half dead with fear, he kept insisting that at any moment she would be there, eager for revenge, whether she armed her slaves or roused the soldiers, or made her way to the senate and people, charging him with the shipwreck, her wound and the killing of her friends: what help did he have against that?
Unless Burrus and Seneca could offer some; he had had them woken and summoned at once, and it is uncertain whether they had known beforehand as well.
So there was a long silence from both of them, either so as not to dissuade him in vain, or because they believed things had come to such a point that, unless Agrippina were forestalled, Nero must perish.
Then Seneca was quicker to act only to this extent, that he looked round at Burrus and asked whether the soldiers should be ordered to carry out the killing.
Burrus replied that the praetorians were bound by oath to the whole house of the Caesars and, remembering Germanicus, would dare no violence against his offspring: Anicetus should make good his promises.
Anicetus, without any hesitation, demanded to be put in charge of the crime. At these words Nero declared that on that day he was being given the empire, and that the author of so great a gift was a freedman: he should go quickly and take with him the men most ready to obey orders.
Nero himself, hearing that Agerinus had come as messenger from Agrippina, took the initiative and stage-managed a charge: while Agerinus was delivering his message, he threw a sword down between his feet, then ordered him to be put in chains as if caught in the act, so that he could make up a story that his mother had plotted the emperor's death and, ashamed that her crime had been detected, had taken her own life.
