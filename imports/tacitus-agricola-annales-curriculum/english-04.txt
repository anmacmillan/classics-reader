The city of Rome was ruled from the beginning by kings; freedom and the consulship were established by Lucius Brutus.
Dictatorships were taken up for a limited time; the power of the decemvirs did not last beyond two years, nor did the consular authority of the military tribunes last long.
The domination of Cinna was not long, nor was Sulla's; and the power of Pompey and Crassus quickly passed to Caesar, the armed forces of Lepidus and Antony to Augustus, who took the whole state, exhausted by civil strife, under his command with the title of princeps.
But the successes and failures of the Roman people of old have been recorded by distinguished writers; and fine talents were not lacking to narrate the times of Augustus, until they were deterred by swelling flattery.
The histories of Tiberius, Gaius, Claudius and Nero were falsified out of fear while they were in power, and after their deaths were written under the influence of fresh hatred.
Hence my plan is to relate a little about Augustus, and his last days, then the principate of Tiberius and the rest, without anger or partisanship, the causes of which I keep far from me.
After Brutus and Cassius had been killed there were no longer any armies of the Republic, Pompey had been crushed off Sicily, and with Lepidus stripped of power and Antony killed not even the Julian party had any leader left but Caesar; he laid aside the title of triumvir, presenting himself as consul and content with a tribune's authority to protect the common people; once he had seduced the soldiers with gifts, the people with the grain supply, and everyone with the sweetness of peace, he began to rise step by step and to draw into his own hands the functions of the senate, the magistrates and the laws, with no one opposing him, since the boldest had fallen in battle or in the proscription, and the rest of the nobles, the readier each was for servitude, were raised up with wealth and offices, and having profited from the new order preferred the safety of the present to the dangers of the past.
Nor did the provinces reject this state of affairs, since they distrusted the rule of senate and people because of the rivalries of the powerful and the greed of the magistrates, and the protection of the laws was powerless, disrupted as they were by violence, intrigue and finally money.
