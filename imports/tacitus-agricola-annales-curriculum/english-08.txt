He allowed nothing to be dealt with on the first day of the senate except the last rites of Augustus, whose will, brought in by the Vestal Virgins, named Tiberius and Livia as heirs.
Livia was adopted into the Julian family and the name of Augusta; as heirs in the second degree he had written in his grandsons and great-grandsons, in the third degree the leading men of the state, most of them hated by him, but out of ostentation and for glory with posterity.
His legacies did not exceed what befits a private citizen, except that he gave the people and the plebs forty-three and a half million sesterces, the soldiers of the praetorian cohorts a thousand sesterces each, those of the urban cohorts five hundred, and the legionaries or the cohorts of Roman citizens three hundred sesterces per man.
Then there was a debate about honours; of these, the ones that seemed most remarkable were these: Asinius Gallus proposed that the funeral procession should pass through the triumphal gate, Lucius Arruntius that the titles of the laws he had passed and the names of the peoples he had conquered should be carried in front of it.
Valerius Messala added that the oath of allegiance to Tiberius should be renewed every year; and when Tiberius asked him whether he had put forward that proposal on his instructions, he replied that he had spoken of his own accord, and that in matters concerning the state he would follow no judgement but his own, even at the risk of giving offence: that was the only form of flattery still left.
The senators cry out together that the body should be carried to the pyre on the shoulders of senators.
Caesar excused them from this with arrogant modesty, and warned the people by edict not to repeat what they had once done when with excessive zeal they had disrupted the funeral of the deified Julius, by wanting Augustus to be cremated in the Forum rather than in the Campus Martius, the place appointed for it.
On the day of the funeral soldiers stood by as if on guard, to the great mockery of those who had themselves seen, or had heard from their parents about, that day of servitude still raw and of freedom unsuccessfully regained, when the murder of Caesar the dictator seemed to some the worst of crimes, to others the finest of deeds: now, they said, an old emperor, after a long reign, who had even provided his heirs with the means to use against the state, needed military protection, of course, so that his burial might pass off quietly.
