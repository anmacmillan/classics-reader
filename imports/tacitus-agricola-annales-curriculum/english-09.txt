After this there was much talk about Augustus himself, most people marvelling at trivialities: that the same day had once been the first of his power and the last of his life, and that he had ended his life at Nola in the same house and bedroom as his father Octavius.
People also celebrated the number of his consulships, by which he had equalled Valerius Corvus and Gaius Marius put together; his tribunician power held without a break for thirty-seven years, the title of imperator won twenty-one times, and other honours multiplied or newly created.
But among the thoughtful his life was variously praised or criticised.
Some said that duty towards his father and the needs of the state, in which there was then no room for law, had driven him to civil war, which can be neither prepared nor waged by honourable means.
He had made many concessions to Antony, while taking vengeance on his father's murderers, and many to Lepidus.
After the latter had grown old in idleness and the former had been ruined by his lusts, there had been no other remedy for the divided fatherland than that it should be ruled by one man.
Yet the state had been ordered neither as a monarchy nor as a dictatorship but under the title of princeps; the empire was bounded by the Ocean or by distant rivers; legions, provinces, fleets, everything was linked together; there was justice for citizens, restraint towards allies; the city itself was magnificently adorned; only very few things had been handled by force, so that the rest might have peace.
It was said on the other side: that duty towards his father and the crisis of the state had been used as a pretext; in reality, out of lust for power, veterans had been stirred up by bribery, an army raised by a young man holding no office, a consul's legions corrupted, support for the Pompeian party feigned; soon, when by decree of the senators he had seized the fasces and the rights of a praetor, and Hirtius and Pansa had been killed (whether the enemy carried them off, or poison poured into his wound killed Pansa, and his own soldiers, with Caesar as the contriver of the plot, killed Hirtius), he had taken over the forces of both; the consulship had been extorted from an unwilling senate, and the arms he had received against Antony turned against the state; then the proscription of citizens and the distributions of land, not praised even by those who carried them out.
Granted, the deaths of Cassius and the Bruti had been a sacrifice to his father's feuds, although it is right to set aside private hatreds for the public good; but Pompeius had been deceived by a semblance of peace, Lepidus by a show of friendship; later Antony, lured by the treaties of Tarentum and Brundisium and by marriage to his sister, had paid for that treacherous kinship with his death.
Peace, no doubt, after this, but a bloody one: the disasters of Lollius and of Varus, and the killing at Rome of men such as Varro, Egnatius and Iullus.
Nor was his private life spared: Nero's wife taken from him, and the pontiffs consulted in mockery whether, with a child conceived but not yet born, she could lawfully marry; the extravagance of Quintus Tedius and Vedius Pollio; finally Livia, a grievous mother to the state, a grievous stepmother to the house of the Caesars.
Nothing had been left for honouring the gods, since he wanted himself worshipped in temples and in the image of deities by flamens and priests.
Not even Tiberius had been adopted as successor out of affection or concern for the state, but, because he had seen through his arrogance and cruelty, Augustus had sought glory for himself from the most unfavourable comparison.
For a few years earlier, when Augustus was asking the senators once more to grant Tiberius tribunician power, he had, although in a complimentary speech, dropped some remarks about his bearing, dress and habits, which he reproached him for while seeming to excuse them.
For the rest, once the burial had been completed in the customary way, a temple and divine worship are decreed.
