In the consulship of Gaius Vipstanus and Gaius Fonteius, Nero no longer put off a crime he had long been contemplating: the length of his reign had hardened his boldness, and he burned more fiercely every day with love for Poppaea, who, with no hope of marriage for herself and of Octavia's divorce while Agrippina was alive, would reproach the emperor with constant accusations, sometimes in jest, and call him a ward, who, subject to other people's orders, lacked not only power but even his freedom.
Why, she asked, was her marriage being put off? Her looks, no doubt, did not please him, nor her ancestors with their triumphs.
Or was it her fertility and her honest heart? The fear was that as his wife she would at least reveal the wrongs done to the senators and the people's anger at his mother's arrogance and greed.
But if Agrippina could tolerate no daughter-in-law except one hostile to her son, let her be given back to her marriage with Otho: she would go anywhere in the world, where she might hear of the insults to the emperor rather than witness them, caught up in his dangers.
These words and others like them, which struck home through the adulteress's tears and skill, no one tried to stop, since everyone wanted the mother's power broken and no one believed that the son's hatred would go as far as her murder.
Cluvius records that Agrippina, in her passion to keep her power, went so far that at midday, at the time when Nero was growing heated with wine and feasting, she more than once offered herself to him in his drunkenness, dressed up and ready for incest; and that when those closest to them were already noticing wanton kisses and caresses that heralded the outrage, Seneca sought help from a woman against a woman's allurements, and the freedwoman Acte was sent in, who, anxious both for her own danger and for Nero's disgrace, was to report that the incest was common knowledge, since his mother boasted of it, and that the soldiers would not tolerate the rule of a sacrilegious emperor.
Fabius Rusticus records that this was desired not by Agrippina but by Nero, and that it was foiled by the cunning of the same freedwoman.
But the other authors too have given the same account as Cluvius, and tradition leans this way, whether Agrippina really conceived such monstrous wickedness in her mind, or whether the planning of a novel lust seemed more credible in a woman who in her girlhood had committed adultery with Lepidus in the hope of power, who with the same greed had sunk so low as to submit to the pleasure of Pallas, and who had been trained for every outrage by her marriage to her uncle.
